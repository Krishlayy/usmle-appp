Q1.  Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

Clinical vignette

A 24-year-old African American woman presents with 4 months of progressive fatigue, intermittent fevers, joint pain in her hands and knees, and a facial rash that worsens with sun exposure. Answer: D

Concept explanation

Picture a security system that decides you, the homeowner, are the intruder. The alarms go off everywhere at once: skin, joints, kidneys, blood vessels, blood cells. This overreactive security system is lupus. Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is the classic multisystem autoimmune disease, where the immune system attacks DNA, RNA, and other parts of the cell nucleus. Since nuclei sit in every cell, the damage shows up all over the body. The typical patient is a woman of childbearing age, with a female-to-male ratio around 9 to 1, and the disease tends to run more severe in Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients. Part of why it hits women so much harder is that some immune genes that were supposed to be inactivated escape X-inactivation and end up double in number, and estrogen also plays a role in the immune response.



The core problem is loss of tolerance to nuclear antigens. Normally, when a cell dies by apoptosis, macrophages quietly clear away its nuclear contents (DNA, histones, ribonucleoproteins). In lupus that cleanup fails, the debris piles up, and it gets presented to T cells, which then help B cells make antibodies against DNA, histones, Smith antigen, and more. Those antibodies bind their targets and form immune complexes that circulate and lodge in vessel walls, glomeruli, joints, skin, and serous membranes. At the systemic level this is a Type III hypersensitivity disease. The complexes fix complement, which is why C3 and C4 fall together during a flare as they get used up, as they pull in neutrophils that further damage tissue. A few features also arise type II (direct antibody) mechanism, such as the autoimmune hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia.



It is worth naming the two ways antibodies do the damage here, because lupus uses both. In a type III reaction, the antibody and its target stick together into little clumps called immune complexes that drift through the blood and lodge in the body’s filters, the glomerulus, the joint lining, and small vessel walls, where they switch on complement and pull in neutrophils. In a type II reaction the antibody instead lands straight onto a cell, a red cell or a platelet, and flags it for destruction. Most of lupus runs on the type III route, with a type II sideline that explains the low blood counts.

The clinical presentation is ambiguous, hence the comprehensive diagnostic criteria checklist. The skin shows the photosensitive malar (butterfly) rash that spares the nasolabial folds, discoid lesions, and painless oral ulcers. The joints give a non-erosive arthritis. The serosal surfaces inflame into pleuritis and pericarditis. The kidney is the prognostic linchpin, since lupus nephritis drives much of the long-term outcome. The blood shows cytopenias, the nervous system can produce seizures or psychosis, and lupus overlaps with antiphospholipid syndrome, which adds clotting and pregnancy loss. Another form of lupus, drug-induced lupus is the milder, kidney-sparing, anti-histone-positive cousin that resolves when the offending drug is stopped.



Diagnosis often pairs the clinical picture with serology. A positive ANA at a titer of 1:80 or higher is the entry criterion in the 2019 EULAR/ACR scheme. ANA is very sensitive, since more than 95% of lupus patients have it, but it is not specific, so it also turns up in scleroderma, Sjogren, mixed connective tissue disease, drug-induced lupus, autoimmune hepatitis, and even in some healthy older people. The antibodies that confirm lupus are the specific ones: anti-dsDNA (specific, and its level tracks disease activity, especially nephritis), anti-Smith (the most specific, though it does not track activity), anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB (sicca symptoms and neonatal lupus with congenital heart block), anti-histone (drug-induced lupus), and the antiphospholipid antibodies. 



Treatment uses several drugs alone or together. Hydroxychloroquine is given to nearly everyone because it cuts flares, lowers mortality, and is safe in pregnancy; corticosteroids cover moderate-to-severe disease; and mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide, or rituximab are added for organ-threatening problems like lupus nephritis, with belimumab and anifrolumab as newer add-ons. Sun protection matters, since UV light sets off flares by driving keratinocyte apoptosis and exposing nuclear antigens. This patient's wire-loop deposits and diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis are Class IV lupus nephritis, the most aggressive renal pattern, and need prompt, aggressive immunosuppression to head off kidney failure.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Anti-CCP is the most specific antibody for rheumatoid arthritis and reflects autoimmunity to citrullinated proteins, predicting erosive joint disease. It is rarely positive in lupus. This patient does have synovitis, but lupus arthritis is non-erosive, and the malar rash, nephritis, low complement, anti-dsDNA, and anti-Smith point to lupus rather than RA.

B. Anti-Jo-1 goes with the antisynthetase syndrome in polymyositis and dermatomyositis (myositis, interstitial lung disease, mechanic's hands, Raynaud, and arthritis). Those patients have proximal muscle weakness with a high CK, not the malar rash, serositis, and immune-complex nephritis of lupus.

C.  ANA is positive in this patient and is very sensitive, since over 95% of lupus patients have a positive ANA, but it is not specific. It also shows up in scleroderma, Sjogren, mixed connective tissue disease, drug-induced lupus, autoimmune hepatitis, and in a small share of healthy older people. ANA is the screening test, useful to rule the disease out when negative, while the specific antibodies confirm it; the question asks for the antibody that both confirms lupus and tracks activity, which ANA does not.

D. Anti-dsDNA is highly specific for lupus, at roughly 95%, and it is one of only two markers whose levels track disease activity (the other is complement consumption, i.e. decreasing levels of C3 and C4). When a patient flares, anti-dsDNA usually rises as complement drops. It is especially tied to lupus nephritis, since the antibody binds DNA in the glomerular basement membrane and drives immune-complex deposition. This patient's wire-loop deposits and active nephritis make anti-dsDNA the marker that matters most, both for diagnosis and for following the disease.

E. Anti-Scl-70 (anti-topoisomerase I) goes with diffuse cutaneous scleroderma, which thickens the skin of the trunk and brings interstitial lung disease and renal crisis. It does not cause the malar rash, immune-complex glomerulonephritis, or low complement of lupus.

Q2.  Drug-Induced Lupus

Clinical vignette

A 66-year-old man with long-standing hypertension presents with 6 weeks of low-grade fevers, diffuse joint pain, and pleuritic chest pain that worsens when lying flat. His regimen was adjusted 4 months ago, when hydralazine was added for to control his blood pressure. Examination shows a pericardial friction rub and mild symmetric arthralgias of the wrists and knees without deformity; there is no malar rash, oral ulceration, or alopecia. Laboratory studies: hemoglobin 13.1 g/dL, white blood cells 6,400/μL, platelet count 240,000/μL, serum creatinine 0.9 mg/dL. Urinalysis is normal with no proteinuria or cellular casts. ANA is positive at 1:640 (homogeneous). Anti-histone antibody is strongly positive. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies are negative. C3 and C4 are normal. Which of the following findings best distinguishes this patient's condition from systemic lupus erythematosus?

Answer choices

A

Positive anti-Smith antibody

✓

⊘

D

Positive anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibody

⊘

E

High-titer anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies with low C3 and C4

⊘

Answer: B

Concept explanation

Drug-induced lupus (DIL) is a lupus-like illness set off by a medication that fades once the drug is stopped. Unlike idiopathic lupus, which favors young women, DIL has no strong female predisposition and tends to appear in older patients, simply because they are the ones taking the culprit drugs for heart disease, blood pressure, and arrhythmias. It runs milder than true lupus, and the picture is mostly constitutional and serosal: fever, malaise, joint pain, muscle aches, and serositis (pleuritis with pleuritic chest pain, pericarditis with a friction rub).



The most useful contrast with true lupus is what DIL spares. It spares the kidneys and the central nervous system, so there is no significant nephritis and no cerebritis, which is why a patient with serositis and arthralgias but a clean urinalysis and a normal neurologic exam fits DIL. The malar rash and oral ulcers that mark idiopathic lupus are usually absent or mild as well.



Almost all DIL patients are ANA positive, and more than 95% have anti-histone antibodies, the hallmark of the disease. The antibodies that define idiopathic lupus are usually missing: anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith are negative, and complement is normal, because DIL does not build the DNA-anti-DNA immune complexes that consume C3 and C4 in true lupus nephritis. There is one exception, lupus induced from TNF-alpha inhibitors can produce anti-dsDNA antibodies.



Histones are the spool-shaped proteins that DNA winds around to pack itself into the nucleus, so a length of DNA wrapped on its histone spool is called a nucleosome. In drug-induced lupus the antibodies are aimed at these histone spools rather than at bare double-stranded DNA, and that target is the clue that separates it from ordinary lupus, where anti-dsDNA leads.



The drugs which cause lupus are frequently tested. The three most important ones to know are the, “HIP drugs” hydralazine, isoniazid, and procainamide (highest risk); others include minocycline, quinidine, methyldopa, chlorpromazine, and the anti-TNF biologics. Several are cleared by acetylation in the liver, so slow acetylators build up the drug and are more prone to the syndrome, which holds for both hydralazine and procainamide, and hydralazine DIL also tracks with HLA-DR4. The likely mechanism is a mix of broken tolerance, since procainamide and hydralazine inhibit DNA methylation (remember: methylation makes DNA mute) in T cells and pushes them toward autoreactivity, with chemical modification of self-antigens. The treatment is primarily withdrawal of the offending drug. Symptoms settle over days to weeks, though the autoantibodies can linger for months, and a short course of NSAIDs or low-dose corticosteroids is used for the management of serositis in the meantime. 

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Anti-Smith is highly specific for idiopathic lupus and is not part of DIL; finding it would argue for true lupus instead. In DIL the defining antibody is anti-histone, and anti-Smith is absent.

B. Anti-histone antibodies appear in more than 95% of DIL cases and are the serologic hallmark, and the illness spares the kidneys and central nervous system. With normal complement and negative anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith, that pattern separates DIL from idiopathic lupus cleanly, and it matters because the fix is to stop the offending drug rather than start long-term immunosuppression.

C. Anti-centromere goes with limited cutaneous scleroderma (CREST). It has no role in DIL and does not explain serositis, joint pain, or anti-histone positivity.

D. Anti-CCP is the most specific antibody for rheumatoid arthritis and predicts erosive joint disease. This patient has non-deforming joint pain without erosions in a drug-triggered serositis syndrome, so RA and anti-CCP are the wrong frame.

E.This pattern describes idiopathic lupus, not DIL. High-titer anti-dsDNA with low C3 and C4 reflects active immune-complex disease and tracks with lupus nephritis, the renal involvement DIL spares. The one caveat is that anti-TNF-induced lupus can show anti-dsDNA, but this patient's hydralazine exposure, serositis, normal complement, and clean urinalysis fit classic anti-histone DIL.

Q3.  Antiphospholipid Syndrome

Clinical vignette

A 31-year-old woman presents with acute left calf swelling and pain; Doppler ultrasonography confirms a deep venous thrombosis. Her history is notable for two unexplained second-trimester pregnancy losses and a transient ischemic attack at age 29. Examination shows a lacy, violaceous, net-like discoloration over both thighs. Laboratory studies: platelet count 96,000/μL, prothrombin time normal, and a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 58 seconds that FAILS to correct when the patient's plasma is mixed 1:1 with normal pooled plasma. A rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test is falsely positive. Anti-cardiolipin IgG and anti-β2-glycoprotein I IgG antibodies are strongly positive and remain positive on repeat testing 12 weeks later. Which of the following best explains the prolonged aPTT in this patient who has a clearly prothrombotic clinical course?

Answer choices

A

A clotting-factor deficiency that corrects upon mixing with normal plasma

⊘

B

Consumption of clotting factors from disseminated intravascular coagulation

⊘

C

Vitamin K deficiency impairing factors II, VII, IX, and X

⊘

D

Von Willebrand factor deficiency reducing platelet adhesion

⊘

E

An antiphospholipid antibody (lupus anticoagulant) interferes with phospholipid-dependent coagulation assays in vitro while promoting thrombosis in vivo

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

In antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) there is an antibody that makes blood look like it cannot clot, so the aPTT is elevated, but inside the body it does the opposite and drives clotting in arteries and veins. That paradox is the heart of antiphospholipid syndrome, an acquired autoimmune clotting disorder caused by antibodies against phospholipid-binding proteins, mainly beta-2-glycoprotein I. Three antibodies define it: the lupus anticoagulant, anti-cardiolipin, and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I. APS can stand alone (primary) or come with another disease, most often lupus (secondary).



Clinically it is thrombosis plus pregnancy loss. Patients get venous clots (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism), arterial clots (stroke, TIA, or myocardial infarction in a young person), and recurrent miscarriage, classically three or more early losses or one loss at or beyond 10 weeks from placental thrombosis. Other clues are livedo reticularis, thrombocytopenia, and cardiac valve vegetations. Diagnosis requires a clinical event plus a persistently positive antibody, confirmed on repeat testing at least 12 weeks apart, because transient antiphospholipid antibodies can appear after infections without causing disease.



Two lab investigations are frequently tested. The first is the lupus anticoagulant paradox: the antibody binds the phospholipid used in clotting assays and increases the aPTT in the tube, while causing clots in the body. The name is misleading, because it is pro-clotting rather than anticoagulant and is not specific to lupus. Since the cause is an inhibitor and not a missing factor, the prolonged aPTT does not correct when you mix the patient's plasma 1:1 with normal plasma; a true factor deficiency would correct because the normal plasma supplies the missing factor. The second point is that cardiolipin is the antigen used in syphilis screening, so APS classically gives a biologic false-positive VDRL or RPR.



The reason these antibodies clot in the body rather than thin the blood is that beta-2-glycoprotein I normally sits quietly on cell surfaces; once antibodies coat it they switch on the lining of blood vessels and the platelets, tip off complement, and block the natural brakes on clotting such as protein C. In a test tube there are no cells for any of that to happen on, so all the antibody does is soak up the phospholipid the assay needs, which drags out the clotting time.



Treatment is anticoagulation. A patient with a clot gets lifelong warfarin to an INR of 2 to 3, with a higher target after arterial events. In pregnancy the regimen switches to low-molecular-weight heparin plus low-dose aspirin, because warfarin is teratogenic. Direct oral anticoagulants are less effective in APS, especially in triple-positive patients (positive for all three antibodies), who carry the highest clotting risk. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, with widespread small-vessel thrombosis and multiorgan failure, is rare but often fatal and is treated aggressively with anticoagulation, steroids, plasma exchange, and sometimes IVIG. The picture to recognize on a stem is a young patient with both arterial and venous clots, fetal losses, livedo, a low platelet count, a long aPTT that does not correct, and a false-positive syphilis test.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. A clotting-factor deficiency corrects on a 1:1 mix with normal plasma, because the normal plasma supplies what is missing, and it causes bleeding rather than clotting. This patient's aPTT does not correct, which points to an inhibitor, and the problem is thrombosis, not hemorrhage.

B. DIC consumes platelets and clotting factors, so it usually prolongs both the PT and the aPTT, drops the fibrinogen, and raises the D-dimer, typically in an acutely ill, hospitalized patient. This patient has a normal PT, a stable outpatient course, and a specific antibody profile, not a consumptive coagulopathy.

C. Vitamin K deficiency knocks out factors II, VII, IX, and X and prolongs the PT first (factor VII has the shortest half-life), and it causes bleeding. It corrects on mixing and does not produce antiphospholipid antibodies or a false-positive RPR.

D. Von Willebrand disease impairs platelet adhesion and causes mucocutaneous bleeding, sometimes with a mildly long aPTT from a low factor VIII. It corrects on mixing and is a bleeding disorder, so it cannot explain recurrent thrombosis, fetal loss, or antiphospholipid antibodies.

E. The lupus anticoagulant binds the phospholipid in clotting assays and stretches the aPTT, while in the body it promotes thrombosis. Because it acts as an inhibitor rather than a missing factor, the prolonged aPTT does not correct on a 1:1 mixing study. That fits this patient with a deep vein thrombosis, a prior TIA, recurrent fetal loss, livedo reticularis, a low platelet count, a false-positive RPR, and persistently positive anti-cardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I.

Q4.  Rheumatoid Arthritis

Clinical vignette

A 47-year-old woman, a current smoker with a 30-pack-year history, presents with a 6-month history of progressive symmetric pain and swelling in the small joints of both hands. She reports morning stiffness lasting approximately 90 minutes, which improves slowly with activity. She has noticed difficulty making a fist and gripping objects. Examination shows symmetric synovitis of bilateral wrists, metacarpophalangeal joints (2nd through 5th), and proximal interphalangeal joints; the distal interphalangeal joints are spared. Two firm, non-tender subcutaneous nodules are palpable over the extensor surfaces of her elbows. Laboratory studies: ESR 64 mm/hr, CRP 28 mg/L, rheumatoid factor positive at 180 IU/mL (normal <14), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies positive at 240 U/mL (highly elevated). Hand radiographs show periarticular osteopenia and early marginal erosions at the second and third MCP joints. Which of the following best explains how this patient's smoking history contributed to the development of her disease?

Answer choices

⊘

B

~

✓

D

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Rheumatoid arthritis is a unique autoimmune disease in which every morning an inflammatory crew shows up at the knuckles, swells them, and refuses to leave for an hour or more. Eventually the cartilage is gone, the joint capsule is eaten away, and the fingers start drifting sideways. That is rheumatoid arthritis: a chronic, symmetric, erosive arthritis of the small joints, mainly the MCPs, PIPs, and wrists, that spares the distal interphalangeal joints. Osteoarthritis does the reverse and hammers the DIPs. Morning stiffness lasting more than an hour is a hallmark, whereas in osteoarthritis the stiffness eases within minutes of moving.



Over time the chronic synovitis produces the classic hand deformities: ulnar deviation of the fingers, swan-neck and boutonniere deformities, and a Z-shaped thumb. The disease is systemic and reaches well beyond the joints. Rheumatoid nodules form over pressure points, and extra-articular features include interstitial lung disease, pleural effusions with low glucose, pericarditis, scleritis, Felty syndrome (rheumatoid arthritis with splenomegaly and neutropenia), secondary Sjogren, a higher cardiovascular risk, and AA amyloidosis from chronic inflammation. The cervical spine can be involved at C1-C2 (atlantoaxial subluxation).



The central aspect in RA is citrullination. Now and then one of own proteins gets a small chemical edit in which an arginine is converted to a citrulline by the enzyme PAD (peptidylarginine deiminase). Usually this happens during apoptosis or inflammation and the immune system ignores it. But people who carry the HLA-DR4 shared epitope have an MHC class II groove with a positively charged pocket that binds citrullinated peptides unusually well. Once those peptides are presented on HLA-DR4, autoreactive T cells switch on, help B cells, and the B cells make anti-CCP antibodies that bind citrullinated proteins in the joints, fix complement, and recruit neutrophils, keeping the synovium inflamed. TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-1 drive the cascade, which is why blocking them works.



A quick refresher on why the HLA type matters: class II HLA molecules, of which HLA-DR is one, are the trays that antigen-presenting cells use to hold up a peptide for inspection by CD4+ helper T cells. The shared-epitope version of HLA-DR4 happens to be a tray shaped to grip citrullinated peptides well, so it shows them off to T cells more readily, and that is the genetic half of the two-hit setup.



Smoking is the major environmental trigger, because cigarette smoke ramps up PAD activity in the lungs and citrullinates airway proteins. In someone with HLA-DR4, that lung citrullination is the first hit that breaks tolerance: the proteins are presented to T cells, anti-CCP forms, and the antibodies eventually find citrullinated targets in the joints. A smoker who also carries HLA-DR4 has roughly 20 times the RA risk of a nonsmoker without it. Anti-CCP appears years before any joint symptoms and can be found in stored blood long before disease, and it is both more specific than rheumatoid factor (about 95% versus 80%) and a predictor of erosive disease; rheumatoid factor is an IgM antibody against the Fc portion of IgG, more sensitive but less specific. The inflamed synovium piles up into pannus, a mass of synovial cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages that erodes cartilage and bone at the joint margins, which shows up as marginal erosions and periarticular osteopenia on X-ray.



Treatment starts with methotrexate, often with hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine, and steps up to biologics when that is not enough: TNF inhibitors (etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab), the IL-6 receptor antibody tocilizumab, B-cell depletion with rituximab, T-cell costimulation blockade with abatacept, or JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib). The aim is early, tight control to prevent erosions, the treat-to-target approach. Quitting smoking is the most important non-drug step and clearly improves the response to treatment.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Type I hypersensitivity, with mast cells and IgE cross-linking, drives allergy and anaphylaxis, not a chronic erosive arthritis. RA does not run on mast cells, IgE, or histamine; its antibodies are IgG anti-CCP and IgM rheumatoid factor.

B.  B is right that smoking causes oxidative stress but wrong about the mechanism of RA. The disease is not driven by direct oxidative damage to cartilage; that idea fits osteoarthritis better. The autoimmune trigger in RA is citrullination and presentation on HLA-DR4, and once anti-CCP forms the synovial inflammation erodes cartilage and bone, but the trigger is immunologic rather than direct chemical wear.

C. This is a gene-environment interaction. Smoking citrullinates proteins in the lungs of every smoker by turning up PAD, but only people with the HLA-DR4 shared epitope have an MHC II groove that presents those peptides well to T cells. The combination drives anti-CCP production years before the joints hurt. Anti-CCP is the most specific RA antibody and predicts erosive disease. It is the classic two-hit model: a genetic susceptibility plus an environmental trigger, and this patient is both a smoker and HLA-DR4 positive.

D. does change thymic function and circulating immune cells, but it does not selectively wipe out regulatory T cells. The dominant smoking-to-RA link is PAD upregulation and airway citrullination, not thymic involution or Treg loss.

E. NK cells are part of innate immunity and do not cause autoimmune arthritis. Viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B or C, HIV) is usually a self-limited symmetric polyarthritis without the marginal erosions, anti-CCP, or chronic progressive course seen here, and smoking does not act through this route.

Q5.  Sjögren Syndrome

Clinical vignette

A 52-year-old woman presents with 9 months of progressive dry eyes (foreign body sensation, especially in the morning) and dry mouth (difficulty swallowing dry foods, frequent need to sip water). She has had three episodes of dental caries in the past year despite good oral hygiene. Examination shows bilateral parotid gland enlargement, decreased salivary pool, and Schirmer test of 3 mm in both eyes (normal >10 mm). Laboratory studies: ANA positive at 1:640, anti-Ro (SSA) antibody strongly positive, anti-La (SSB) antibody positive, rheumatoid factor positive at 92 IU/mL. Minor salivary gland (lip) biopsy shows a focal lymphocytic infiltrate with a focus score of 3 (≥1 focus per 4 mm² is positive). She is otherwise well. She is concerned because her sister had a similar condition and was later diagnosed with parotid lymphoma. Which of the following best describes the increased malignancy risk in this patient?

Answer choices

A

40-fold increased risk of parotid MALT lymphoma (extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma)

✓

B

5-fold increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue

⊘

C

3-fold increased risk of acute myeloid leukemia

⊘

D

10-fold increased risk of ovarian cancer

⊘

E

No increased malignancy risk above the general population

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Sjogren syndrome is what happens when the immune system goes after your tear and saliva factories. Lymphocytes move into the lacrimal and salivary glands, settle in, and stay, and over time they replace working gland tissue with lymphoid follicles. The result is the sicca syndrome: dry, gritty eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca, which can scar the cornea) and a dry mouth that makes dry food hard to swallow (xerostomia, with runaway dental caries and frequent oral thrush). Patients drink water with every meal and keep a glass by the bed. On exam you find parotid enlargement, often on both sides, a dry mouth, and a low Schirmer test, where the filter paper wets less than 5 mm in 5 minutes.

Workup combines symptoms, exam, and serology. The main antibodies are anti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB), found in roughly 60 to 80% and 30 to 50% of patients, and rheumatoid factor and ANA are often positive too. Both Ro and La also occur in lupus, but the tie to Sjogren is strong. A labial (lip) biopsy showing focal lymphocytic sialadenitis with a focus score of 1 or more is highly specific. Sjogren can be primary, as in this patient, or secondary to another autoimmune disease, most often rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, or scleroderma; about half of rheumatoid arthritis patients eventually develop secondary Sjogren.



Two anti-Ro points are important to remember. It crosses the placenta and can cause neonatal lupus, a transient rash and, more dangerously, congenital heart block from antibodies attacking the fetal conduction system, so an anti-Ro-positive woman is monitored in pregnancy. And it links to subacute cutaneous lupus and photosensitive rashes. Beyond the dryness, Sjogren has systemic features: a non-erosive arthritis, Raynaud, interstitial lung disease, a distal (type 1) renal tubular acidosis from interstitial nephritis, peripheral neuropathy, and cutaneous vasculitis with palpable purpura, sometimes with cryoglobulins.



One of the complications of this disease is increased risk of lymphoma, specifically MALT lymphoma (extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma) of the salivary glands, especially the parotid. The underlying mechanism is that chronic immune stimulation keeps B cells proliferating in a germinal-center-like environment, mutations accumulate, and one clone can turn malignant. The risk runs many times that of the general population, the highest of any rheumatologic disease. Signs that should raise suspicion include a persistent or asymmetric parotid swelling, B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), a falling complement, cryoglobulinemia, palpable purpura, and an enlarged spleen.



To see why years of dryness can end in cancer, picture the germinal center, the training camp inside lymphoid tissue where B cells deliberately mutate their antibody genes to sharpen their fit and then compete to survive. A little controlled mutation is normal, but when inflamed glands run that camp non-stop for years the odds catch up: one B-cell clone picks up the wrong mutation, stops taking orders, and grows into a MALT lymphoma.



Dryness is managed with supportive care (artificial tears, pilocarpine or cevimeline to coax more secretion from the remaining glands, sugar-free gum, and diligent fluoride dental care). Hydroxychloroquine helps the fatigue and joint pain, and rituximab or other immunosuppressants are reserved for systemic disease such as vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, or neuropathy.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Sjogren carries a large increase in MALT lymphoma risk, especially in the parotid, on the order of 15 to 40 times the general population, the highest lymphoma risk of any rheumatologic disease. The driver is chronic B-cell stimulation in the inflamed glands, which over years can move from polyclonal hyperplasia to a monoclonal expansion to frank MALT lymphoma. A persistent asymmetric parotid, falling complement, cryoglobulinemia, palpable purpura, or B symptoms should prompt a workup, and surveillance is recommended.

B. Sjogren patients do get more oral disease, like rampant caries and thrush from the dry mouth, but tongue squamous cell carcinoma is not the dominant cancer; which is more associated with tobacco, alcohol, and HPV. The signature Sjogren malignancy is parotid MALT lymphoma, not tongue cancer, and the magnitude (5-fold) and site here are wrong.

C. Sjogren is not linked to myeloid cancers. Its cancer association is B-cell lymphoid (MALT lymphoma, then diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, then other B-cell lymphomas). AML risk relates more to prior chemotherapy, myelodysplasia, and certain genetic syndromes, not to autoimmune sicca disease.

D. Ovarian cancer risk is raised in BRCA1/BRCA2 carriers, Lynch syndrome, endometriosis, and with long ovulatory histories. Sjogren has no specific tie to it; the over-represented cancer is B-cell lymphoma.

E. The increased lymphoma risk in Sjogren is well established and clinically important rather than absent, so saying there is no excess malignancy risk is incorrect. Missing it delays a lymphoma diagnosis, which is why a persistent one-sided parotid swelling in a Sjogren patient goes to biopsy without delay.

Q6.  Diffuse Systemic Sclerosis (Scleroderma)

Clinical vignette

A 48-year-old woman with diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis (skin thickening involving trunk, face, and extremities for 18 months; positive anti-Scl-70 antibody) is admitted from clinic with new-onset severe headache, blurred vision, and dyspnea over 48 hours. She has been on prednisone 25 mg daily for the past 6 weeks for a flare of polyarthritis. Vital signs: BP 224/132 mmHg, HR 104/min. Examination shows grade 3 hypertensive retinopathy with hemorrhages and cotton-wool spots. Laboratory studies: serum creatinine 3.8 mg/dL (baseline 0.9 mg/dL), hemoglobin 8.4 g/dL, platelet count 88,000/μL, schistocytes on peripheral blood smear, LDH 920 U/L (elevated), haptoglobin <10 mg/dL (decreased). Urinalysis shows 2+ proteinuria, occasional red blood cells, no casts. Plasma renin activity is markedly elevated. Which of the following is the most appropriate first-line treatment for this patient's acute presentation?

Answer choices

A

Pulse intravenous methylprednisolone

⊘

B

Cyclophosphamide intravenous induction therapy

⊘

C

Intravenous nitroprusside infusion

~

D

⊘

E

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Scleroderma is essentially the skin and organs slowly turning into leather, and it has three components: vascular damage (small vessels constrict and get injured, giving early Raynaud and later organ ischemia), fibrosis (fibroblasts overproduce collagen and stiffen skin and organs), and autoimmunity (antibodies appear and help sort the subtype). The skin goes shiny and tight, the fingers curl into sclerodactyly, and the face turns mask-like with pursed lips and a beaked nose. There are two main forms. Limited cutaneous disease keeps the skin changes on the face, hands, and feet (CREST), carries the anti-centromere antibody, moves slowly, and tends to kill through pulmonary arterial hypertension. Diffuse cutaneous disease spreads to the trunk and proximal limbs, carries anti-Scl-70 (anti-topoisomerase I), moves faster, and threatens the lungs with interstitial fibrosis and the kidneys with renal crisis, and anti-RNA polymerase III marks the highest renal-crisis risk.



This patient is in scleroderma renal crisis. The small renal vessels (interlobular and arcuate arteries) undergo intimal proliferation and fibrinoid necrosis and narrow down. The drop in renal blood flow switches on the renin-angiotensin system, which pours out renin, generates angiotensin II, tightens the vessels further, and pushes the blood pressure to malignant levels. Red cells shearing through those narrowed vessels produce a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, high LDH, low platelets).



Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia is worth unpacking: micro-angio-pathic just means small-vessel damage, and when red cells are forced through vessels narrowed and roughened by that damage they get sliced into fragments called schistocytes. The shredding spills hemoglobin, so LDH climbs and haptoglobin (the protein that mops up free hemoglobin) is used up, and platelets are consumed along the way.



The triad is sudden severe hypertension (often above 200/120), acute kidney injury with a rising creatinine, and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. The risk factors are the diffuse subset, the anti-RNA polymerase III antibody (the highest risk), early disease in the first 4 years, and high-dose corticosteroids above 15 mg of prednisone a day, which is exactly why this patient on 25 mg developed it. The crisis can even be the first presentation of scleroderma, so it is worth keeping in mind before the skin disease is obvious.



Clinical pearl: the treatment is an ACE inhibitor, and captopril is preferred because it is short-acting and easy to titrate quickly. This is one of the rare places in autoimmune disease where an ACE inhibitor is lifesaving rather than something to avoid (vs. in case of hereditary angioedema). Before ACE inhibitors became standard in the 1980s, scleroderma renal crisis killed about 90% of patients within a year; with prompt treatment that has fallen to around 30 to 40%. It is important to gradually lower the blood pressure (over 72 hours) so the blood supply to the kidneys is not suddenly cut-off. Keep the ACE inhibitor going even if the patient ends up on dialysis, because renal function can recover over months.



Two traps regarding treatment are tested here. First, prophylactic ACE inhibitors do not prevent renal crisis and may worsen outcomes, so they are for treating an active crisis, not preventing one, and high-dose steroids are best avoided in at-risk patients because they can trigger the crisis. Second, the usual autoimmune flare tools (corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, plasmapheresis) are not first-line here and can make things worse; cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate are reserved for the interstitial lung disease of diffuse scleroderma.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. High-dose corticosteroids are a major trigger of scleroderma renal crisis, and this patient developed hers after weeks on 25 mg of prednisone a day. Pulse methylprednisolone would make it worse. The teaching point is to avoid prednisone above 15 mg a day in scleroderma unless absolutely necessary, and then only with great caution.

B. Cyclophosphamide is wrong for the acute crisis, though it is reasonable for the interstitial lung disease of diffuse scleroderma, where cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate are first-line. Cyclophosphamide does nothing for the immediate problem of malignant hypertension and failing kidneys. Control the pressure with an ACE inhibitor first, and consider chronic immunosuppression for other organ problems only after the patient is stable.

C. Nitroprusside is partly right but second-best, a strong arterial vasodilator and will bring the pressure down fast, but it does not target the renin-angiotensin axis driving the crisis, it carries cyanide toxicity with prolonged use, and it is not disease-specific. The standard is an ACE inhibitor first, adding a calcium channel blocker or other agents if the pressure will not come down on a maxed-out ACE inhibitor. Nitroprusside might bridge a hypertensive emergency until the ACE inhibitor takes hold, but it is not the treatment for the disease.

D. Plasmapheresis with fresh frozen plasma treats thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, which can look similar with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, low platelets, and acute kidney injury. But TTP also brings fever and neurologic signs and comes from ADAMTS13 deficiency. In scleroderma renal crisis the hemolysis is mechanical shearing of red cells through narrowed renal vessels, not antibody-driven platelet clumping, so plasmapheresis does not address the real problem, which is renin-angiotensin-driven vasoconstriction.

E. Captopril is first-line for scleroderma renal crisis. The renin-angiotensin system is the engine behind the hypertension, and an ACE inhibitor blocks angiotensin II production directly. Captopril's short half-life lets you titrate the pressure down over 72 hours without dropping renal perfusion too fast. Keep it going even if dialysis becomes necessary, since long-term ACE inhibition can let kidney function recover over months. This is the rare setting where an ACE inhibitor is lifesaving in autoimmune disease.

Answer: B

Concept explanation

If diffuse scleroderma is the aggressive sibling that scars the lungs and threatens the kidneys, limited cutaneous scleroderma is the slow one whose skin stays distal while its blood vessels quietly go after the lungs. In limited disease the skin thickening stops below the elbows and knees, plus the face, and leaves the trunk and proximal limbs alone. The features make the CREST mnemonic: calcinosis cutis (firm calcium nodules in the fingertips), Raynaud (often present for years before anything else), esophageal dysmotility (dysphagia and reflux from smooth-muscle fibrosis), sclerodactyly (tight, shiny finger skin), and telangiectasias. The defining antibody is anti-centromere.



Compare that with diffuse cutaneous scleroderma, it involves skin on the trunk and proximal limbs, a rapid course, anti-Scl-70 antibody, interstitial lung disease, and the risk of renal crisis (most with anti-RNA polymerase III and high-dose steroids). The antibody is specific to each subtype of scleroderma; anti-centromere goes with limited disease and pulmonary arterial hypertension, anti-Scl-70 with diffuse disease and interstitial lung disease, and anti-RNA polymerase III with diffuse disease and renal crisis.



The leading cause of death in limited disease is pulmonary arterial hypertension. Over years the proliferative vasculopathy narrows the pulmonary arterioles, raises pulmonary pressures, and strains the right heart, giving exertional dyspnea, a loud P2, a right ventricular heave, and eventually right heart failure. Because it creeps up quietly, patients with limited scleroderma should get annual echocardiography and pulmonary function tests. An isolated drop in the diffusing capacity (DLCO) out of proportion to lung volumes is an early sign of PAH, and right heart catheterization confirmatory.



The DLCO, or diffusing capacity, is just a breathing test of how easily gas crosses from the air sacs into the blood. In pulmonary arterial hypertension the lung tissue itself is fine but the small pulmonary vessels are choked, so gas transfer falls even though lung volumes stay normal, and that mismatch, a low DLCO with preserved volumes, is the early tell.



Treatment targets the pulmonary vessels with endothelin-receptor antagonists (bosentan, ambrisentan), PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil), and prostacyclin analogues. Raynaud’s is managed by avoiding cold temperatures and calcium channel blockers, reflux is managed with proton-pump inhibitors. In the diffuse subset the lungs are threatened mainly by fibrosis rather than vascular disease, which is treated with immunosuppression (mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide) and antifibrotics (nintedanib) instead.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Renal crisis belongs to diffuse disease and goes with anti-RNA polymerase III and high-dose steroids. This patient has limited disease (distal skin only, anti-centromere positive, anti-RNA polymerase III negative) and a normal renal picture, so renal crisis is not the main threat.

B. Pulmonary arterial hypertension is the leading cause of death in limited cutaneous scleroderma. It comes from a slowly progressive pulmonary vasculopathy rather than scarring, which is why this patient shows pulmonary hypertension and right heart strain (loud P2, RV heave, high RVSP, dilated RV) with clear lung tissue on CT. Anti-centromere positivity points to the limited subset, where isolated PAH predominates, and explains the need for lifelong surveillance.

C.  C is partly relevant but not the dominant process here. Interstitial lung disease can occur in limited disease, but it is far more a feature of diffuse disease with anti-Scl-70. This patient's CT shows no real fibrosis, and the physiology points to pulmonary vascular disease, so PAH is the better answer.

D. Crescentic, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis comes from ANCA-associated vasculitis and anti-GBM disease, not limited scleroderma. Scleroderma injures the kidney through renal-crisis vasculopathy in the diffuse subset, not crescentic immune glomerulonephritis.

E. Parotid MALT lymphoma is the signature cancer of Sjogren, driven by chronic salivary-gland B-cell stimulation. It has no specific tie to limited scleroderma and does not explain this patient's pulmonary-vascular picture.

That is mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD), an overlap of lupus, systemic sclerosis, and polymyositis tied together by one finding: a high-titer anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP) antibody. When the picture mixes lupus-like, scleroderma-like, and myositis-like features and the anti-U1 RNP is strongly positive, MCTD is the likely diagnosis.

Lastly, some features of polymyositis like proximal muscle weakness with a raised creatine kinase, as in this patient, can also be seen. Anti-U1 RNP sounds cryptic but the target is tidy: U1 ribonucleoprotein is one of the little machines in the nucleus that splice messenger RNA, built from a short U1 RNA bundled with proteins. In mixed connective tissue disease the immune system makes antibodies against that splicing particle, and it is the sheer height of the titer, not merely its presence, that defines the overlap syndrome.



As in limited scleroderma, the leading cause of death is pulmonary arterial hypertension, so it is watched for with echocardiography. D. Anti-Jo-1 marks the antisynthetase syndrome (myositis with interstitial lung disease, mechanic's hands, Raynaud, and arthritis). This patient does have myositis, but the defining antibody is anti-U1 RNP in a multi-disease overlap, not isolated antisynthetase myositis.

Concept explanation

The shared core of the inflammatory myopathies is that they target the skeletal muscle, so the big muscles near the trunk slowly fail and the patient can no longer climb stairs, get out of a chair, or comb their hairs. Dermatomyositis and polymyositis mainly involve symmetric, painless, proximal weakness with high muscle enzymes (creatine kinase and aldolase). The primary distinction between both is the involvement of skin in the former. Dermatomyositis adds characteristic, sometimes pathognomonic, skin findings: the heliotrope rash (violaceous discoloration of the eyelids), Gottron papules (scaly violaceous papules over the knuckles), the photodistributed shawl and V signs, periungual changes, and mechanic's hands.



The two also differ under the microscope. Dermatomyositis is a humoral, complement-mediated microangiopathy: immune complexes and the membrane-attack complex deposit on the small vessels in muscle and starve the edges of each fascicle, which is why the biopsy shows perifascicular atrophy with perivascular CD4+ and B-cell inflammation, as here. Polymyositis is cell-mediated: CD8+ T cells invade otherwise healthy muscle fibers that have switched on MHC class I, giving an endomysial infiltrate. Both raise the CK and give a myopathic pattern on EMG, but the rash and the perifascicular pattern are characteristic of dermatomyositis.



The membrane-attack complex is the finale of the complement cascade, a ring of proteins (C5b through C9) that punches a pore in a target membrane and bursts it. In dermatomyositis that ring is aimed at the tiny blood vessels feeding the muscle, so the fibers at the edge of each bundle starve first, which is exactly why the biopsy shows perifascicular atrophy.



Several myositis-specific antibodies sharpen the picture and flag complications: anti-Jo-1 and the other antisynthetases mark the antisynthetase syndrome (myositis with interstitial lung disease, mechanic's hands, Raynaud, arthritis, and fever); anti-Mi-2 goes with classic dermatomyositis skin disease and a better prognosis; anti-SRP with severe necrotizing myopathy; anti-MDA5 with clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis and rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease; and anti-TIF1-gamma and anti-NXP2 with a strong cancer link.



The highest-yield point is the cancer association. Adult dermatomyositis, and to a lesser extent polymyositis, carries a real increase in occult malignancy, with ovarian, lung, pancreatic, gastric, and colorectal cancers and non-Hodgkin lymphoma over-represented, and the risk is highest right around the time of diagnosis. So every adult with new dermatomyositis needs age- and risk-appropriate cancer screening, including screening for ovarian cancer in women, and often treating the underlying cancer can improve the myositis itself.



Treatment is with high-dose corticosteroids first, and adding steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, IVIG, or mycophenolate) when it does not respond.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. ACE inhibitors prevent and treat scleroderma renal crisis, which belongs to diffuse systemic sclerosis, not dermatomyositis. There is no renal-crisis equivalent in dermatomyositis or polymyositis, so prophylactic ACE inhibition has no role here.

B. Plasmapheresis is not standard for dermatomyositis. The mainstay is corticosteroids, with steroid-sparing immunosuppressants and IVIG for stubborn cases. Urgent plasma exchange is for other problems like TTP or certain neurologic emergencies, not dermatomyositis.

C. Anti-dsDNA and lupus nephritis belong to lupus. This patient's proximal myopathy with a high CK, heliotrope rash, and Gottron papules is dermatomyositis, and chasing lupus nephritis would miss the cancer association.

D. Adult dermatomyositis is strongly tied to occult cancer, with the risk concentrated around the time of diagnosis, so age- and risk-appropriate screening is part of the initial workup. Treating an underlying cancer can improve the myositis itself. This patient's classic findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, proximal weakness, perifascicular atrophy) make the cancer search a priority alongside immunosuppression.

E. Corticosteroids are first-line for dermatomyositis, not contraindicated. Long-term high-dose steroids can cause a separate steroid myopathy, but that does not make steroids off-limits in active inflammatory myopathy, and withholding them would leave the disease untreated.

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Ankylosing spondylitis is unique autoimmune disease where the vertebrae of the spine slowly fuse together (bamboo spine). Inflammation where ligaments attach to bone (the entheses) sets off an odd healing response, and the body lays down new bone that bridges the vertebrae and eventually fuses them, so a late X-ray shows the stiff 'bamboo spine.' It is the prototype of the seronegative spondyloarthropathies, which share three things: they hit the spine and sacroiliac joints more than the peripheral ones, they are rheumatoid factor negative (hence seronegative), and they are strongly tied to HLA-B27. The four make the PAIR mnemonic: psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, IBD-associated arthritis, and reactive arthritis.



The characteristic feature is inflammatory back pain, and on history it sounds nothing like mechanical back pain. Mechanical pain, the kind from lifting a box wrong, is worse with activity and better with rest. Inflammatory pain is the opposite: worse in the morning, better with exercise, often waking the patient in the second half of the night, with stiffness lasting over an hour. The typical patient is a young man, peak onset around 20 to 30, with a male-to-female ratio near 3 to 1. Other clues are a positive family history, a reduced Schober test (a measure of lumbar flexion), and reduced chest expansion as the costovertebral joints fuse. Sacroiliitis shows on MRI before it appears on plain x-ray.



The extra-articular features are often tested and follow an 'A' theme. About 30% get acute anterior uveitis (a sudden painful red eye with photophobia, treated with topical steroids), and patients can develop aortic regurgitation with conduction block, apical pulmonary fibrosis, and IgA nephropathy, along with a higher risk of vertebral fracture as the rigid spine turns brittle and AA amyloidosis occurs from chronic inflammation.



HLA-B27 is the strongest known genetic risk factor, positive in about 90% of patients versus 5 to 8% of the general population. It is not a diagnostic test on its own, though, because most B27-positive people never develop the disease, so a positive result alone does not make the diagnosis. Why it predisposes is still debated, but a couple of possible theories are: i) molecular mimicry, where HLA-B27 presents a peptide resembling a gut microbe and triggers autoreactive T cells; ii) the misfolding hypothesis, where HLA-B27 forms misfolded homodimers that drive ER stress and IL-23/Th17 inflammation; and altered gut-microbiome interactions.



HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen) molecules are the human form of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and are responsible for presenting antigens to T cells, enabling adaptive immune responses. Class I HLA (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) is expressed on all nucleated cells and presents endogenous (intracellular) peptides to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, whereas Class II HLA (HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR) is expressed on antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells) and presents exogenous (extracellular) peptides to CD4⁺ helper T cells. HLA genes are highly polymorphic, making them critical in transplant compatibility, susceptibility to certain autoimmune diseases (eg, HLA-B27 with ankylosing spondylitis, HLA-DR3/DR4 with type 1 diabetes), and immune recognition of pathogens. It helps to sort each disease by HLA type, since every wrong answer here is a different HLA type. Class I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, with B27 among them) sit on nearly every cell and show peptides to CD8+ killer T cells. Class II molecules (HLA-DR, HLA-DQ, HLA-DP) sit on antigen-presenting cells and show peptides to CD4+ helper T cells, the group tied to rheumatoid arthritis (DR4), celiac disease (DQ2/DQ8), and lupus (DR3). Ankylosing spondylitis breaks the pattern by hanging off a class I gene, HLA-B27. Refer to the next table to remember HLA subtype and associated diseases.

Clinical vignette

A 38-year-old man presents with 5 months of pain and swelling in the fingers of both hands. He has had scaly plaques on his elbows and scalp since his twenties, and lately the skin behind his ears has flared. On examination, the distal interphalangeal joints of several fingers are swollen and tender, two fingers are diffusely swollen along their whole length like small sausages, and there is pitting and lifting of several fingernails. The lumbar spine moves normally and the sacroiliac joints are not tender. Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies are negative. A radiograph of the hand shows erosion of the tuft of a distal phalanx with a cupped, widened base of the adjacent bone, giving a "pencil-in-cup" appearance. Which of the following radiographic findings is most characteristic of this patient's arthritis?

Answer choices

A

Bridging syndesmophytes producing a "bamboo spine"

⊘

B

"Pencil-in-cup" deformity at the distal interphalangeal joints

✓

C

Chondrocalcinosis of the joint fibrocartilage

⊘

D

Periarticular "punched-out" erosions with overhanging edges

⊘

E

Symmetric marginal erosions with periarticular osteopenia

⊘

Answer: B

Concept explanation

This is a type of seronegative arthritis associated with psoriasis and nail lesions. Around one in five people with psoriasis develops psoriatic arthritis, and in most of them the skin plaques come first, sometimes by years, though about 15% get the joint disease before any obvious skin disease. It belongs to the seronegative spondyloarthropathy family, so rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP are negative, HLA-B27 turns up when the spine is involved, and HLA-Cw6 tracks with the skin disease. The inflammation runs largely on the IL-23/IL-17 (Th17) axis along with TNF, which is exactly why drugs that block those cytokines are useful in both the skin lesions and joint pain.



There are five classic patterns, and a clinical vignette can show any of them: an asymmetric oligoarthritis (the most common), a symmetric polyarthritis that mimics rheumatoid arthritis, a distal interphalangeal (DIP)-predominant pattern, the destructive arthritis mutilans, and an axial pattern with sacroiliitis and spondylitis. Two findings are commonly tested in exam: i) Dactylitis, which is the 'sausage digit,' a whole finger or toe swollen from combined joint and tendon-sheath inflammation, and ii) Enthesitis, which is inflammation where tendons and ligaments insert into bone, classically at the Achilles and plantar fascia. The nails also have following lesions: pitting, oil-drop discoloration, and onycholysis. A minority of patients progress to arthritis mutilans, where bone resorption collapses the digits and the skin folds up into a 'telescoping' finger.



Here is the idea that ties the spondyloarthropathies together: the disease starts at the enthesis, the spot where a tendon or ligament anchors into bone, rather than in the joint lining the way rheumatoid arthritis does. Because the enthesis is a place where the body both tears down and builds up bone, these diseases erode in some spots and grow fresh bone in others at the same time, which is why the films show an odd mix of erosions, fluffy new periosteal bone, and eventually fusion that you do not see in the pure erosions of rheumatoid arthritis.



 Figure 2: “Pencil-in-cup” deformity





The radiograph features are equally important. As the disease erodes the end of one phalanx and the base of the next bone expands, you get the 'pencil-in-cup' deformity (Figure 2), a sharpened pencil sitting in a cup, and it favors the DIP joints. Other clues on film are fluffy periostitis (new bone along the shaft), osteolysis, and joint ankylosis, with axial disease showing asymmetric sacroiliitis and chunky, non-marginal syndesmophytes. Contrast this next to the look-alikes: rheumatoid arthritis gives symmetric marginal erosions and periarticular osteopenia at the MCPs, PIPs, and wrists while sparing the DIPs; osteoarthritis gives DIP osteophytes with Heberden and Bouchard nodes; gout carves punched-out erosions with overhanging sclerotic edges; and CPPD lays calcium down in cartilage as chondrocalcinosis.



Diagnosis is clinical, supported by the CASPAR criteria, and the seronegative labs (negative RF and anti-CCP) help separate it from rheumatoid arthritis. Treatment typically includes NSAIDs, which cover mild peripheral disease; conventional DMARDs such as methotrexate, leflunomide, or sulfasalazine help peripheral joints and skin but do little for axial disease. Moderate-to-severe or axial disease often requires biologics: TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab), IL-12/23 and IL-23 inhibitors (ustekinumab, guselkumab, risankizumab), the oral PDE4 inhibitor apremilast, or JAK inhibitors.



Why each option is right or wrong

A. This describes the late spine in ankylosing spondylitis, where symmetric marginal syndesmophytes bridge adjacent vertebrae into a bamboo spine. Psoriatic arthritis can involve the spine, but its syndesmophytes are asymmetric and chunky, this patient has a normal lumbar exam with nontender sacroiliac joints, and bamboo spine is not the hand finding being asked about.

B. The pencil-in-cup deformity comes from erosion of the distal end of one phalanx with expansion of the base of the adjacent bone, and it favors the DIP joints, with the most dramatic form seen in arthritis mutilans. In a patient with psoriasis, DIP arthritis, dactylitis, and nail pitting, it is the radiographic signature of psoriatic arthritis.

C. In calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (pseudogout), where calcium pyrophosphate lines the fibrocartilage as chondrocalcinosis, often in the knee meniscus or wrist, and the fluid shows rhomboid, positively birefringent crystals. It does not explain DIP arthritis with nail pitting and psoriatic plaques.

D. In chronic tophaceous gout, where urate deposition over years produces periarticular erosions with sclerotic overhanging edges, classically at the first MTP joint, alongside hyperuricemia and negatively birefringent needle-shaped crystals. Gout is crystal-driven and does not give the psoriasis, dactylitis, and pencil-in-cup picture seen here.

E. RA gives symmetric marginal erosions and periarticular osteopenia at the MCPs, PIPs, and wrists while sparing the DIPs, and it is seropositive for RF and anti-CCP. This patient is seronegative with DIP disease, dactylitis, and nail changes, which points away from RA.

Q12.  Reactive Arthritis

Clinical vignette

A 27-year-old man develops pain and swelling in his right knee and left ankle, along with pain at the back of both heels. Three weeks earlier he had a week of bloody diarrhea after a barbecue, which resolved on its own. He also reports burning with urination and red, gritty eyes for the past several days. On examination, the right knee has a moderate effusion, the left Achilles insertion is tender, and the conjunctivae are injected. Arthrocentesis of the knee yields cloudy fluid with a white cell count of 12,000/μL, no crystals, and a Gram stain and culture that are negative. HLA-B27 is positive. Which of the following best explains this patient's arthritis?

Answer choices

A

Direct bacterial seeding of the joint (septic arthritis)

⊘

B

Antibodies against citrullinated peptides attacking the synovium

⊘

C

A sterile, immune-mediated arthritis triggered by a recent gastrointestinal or genitourinary infection

✓

D

Hematogenous spread of Neisseria gonorrhoeae to the joint

~

E

Deposition of monosodium urate crystals in the joint

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Reactive arthritis is the joint reacting to an infection that happened somewhere else and has usually already cleared. It follows one of two kinds of trigger by one to four weeks: a gut infection (Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter, and sometimes Clostridioides difficile) or a genitourinary infection (Chlamydia trachomatis, and occasionally Ureaplasma). The triad of clinical findings is arthritis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis, remembered as 'can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree.' Many patients show only two of the three, so the full set is helpful but not required, and the old eponym 'Reiter syndrome' is no longer in use.



The mechanism is post-infectious and immune-mediated, thought to involve molecular mimicry between microbial and self-antigens, and HLA-B27 is positive in roughly half to three-quarters of patients and predicts more severe and more chronic disease. A major distinction with septic arthritis and reactive arthritis is that the joint itself is sterile. If you tap it, you find an inflammatory effusion but no organism on Gram stain or culture (Chlamydia nucleic acid or antigen can sometimes be detected, but viable bacteria are not seeding the joint).



Molecular mimicry is the leading explanation worth understanding: when the immune system fights the gut or genital infection, some microbial proteins look enough like the body’s own proteins that the primed T cells later turn on self-tissue, especially in joints and entheses. HLA-B27 seems to make this misfire easier by the way it presents those look-alike peptides, which is why the same B27 gene keeps turning up across this whole family of diseases.



Clinically it is an asymmetric oligoarthritis of the lower limbs, often with enthesitis (heel pain at the Achilles or plantar fascia), dactylitis, and inflammatory low back pain or sacroiliitis, the same spondyloarthropathy features shared with psoriatic arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis. The extra-articular findings are worth knowing: conjunctivitis or anterior uveitis, urethritis (which can appear even after a gut trigger), circinate balanitis (shallow ulcers on the glans), keratoderma blennorrhagicum (a crusted, pustular rash on the palms and soles that looks like pustular psoriasis), oral ulcers, and nail changes.



Diagnosis is clinical, anchored by a sterile inflammatory synovial fluid that rules out septic arthritis and shows no crystals to rule out gout. If Chlamydia is found, the patient and partner are treated with antibiotics, which helps the infection even though it does not reliably shorten the arthritis. Most cases resolve within months. NSAIDs are first-line for pain management, while troublesome joints get intra-articular steroids, and persistent disease may need sulfasalazine, methotrexate, or a TNF inhibitor. The most important point to get right on a clinical vignette is recognizing a recent gut or genitourinary infection plus a sterile, asymmetric, lower-limb arthritis in an HLA-B27 patient, rather than chasing an organism inside the joint.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Septic arthritis would show organisms on Gram stain or culture and usually a much higher synovial white cell count, often above 50,000/μL with a high neutrophil percentage, in a single hot joint. This fluid is inflammatory but culture-negative, and the illness involves several joints plus eye and urinary symptoms, which is not how a septic joint behaves.

B. Rheumatoid arthritis, an antibody-driven symmetric small-joint disease with anti-CCP and rheumatoid factor. It is not triggered by a recent gut infection and does not cause urethritis or conjunctivitis.

C. The arthritis is an immune reaction to a recent gut or genitourinary infection, not an infection of the joint itself, which is why the synovial fluid is inflammatory but sterile. The asymmetric lower-limb arthritis, enthesitis, conjunctivitis, urethritis, and HLA-B27 positivity after a bout of dysentery fit reactive arthritis cleanly.

D. Disseminated gonorrhoea is a reasonable thought in a sexually active young man, but disseminated gonococcal infection usually causes a migratory arthritis with tenosynovitis and pustular skin lesions, and cultures aim to recover Neisseria. Here the synovial fluid is sterile and the illness followed a gastrointestinal infection, which points to reactive arthritis rather than gonococcal septic arthritis.

E. Gout produces negatively birefringent, needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals, classically at the first MTP joint in an older patient with hyperuricemia. This aspirate has no crystals, and the postinfectious, multisite, HLA-B27 picture is not gout.

Q13.  Giant Cell Arteritis and Polymyalgia Rheumatica

Clinical vignette

A 72-year-old woman presents with 3 weeks of a new, persistent right-sided headache and pain in her jaw when she chews that forces her to stop midway through a meal. Her scalp is tender when she brushes her hair. This morning she had a brief episode of curtain-like visual loss in the right eye that resolved after a few minutes. For the past 2 months she has also had aching and stiffness in both shoulders and hips, worst in the morning. On examination, the right temporal artery is thickened and tender with a reduced pulse. ESR is 88 mm/hr and CRP is markedly elevated. Muscle strength is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate next step?

Answer choices

A

Withhold treatment until temporal artery biopsy confirms the diagnosis

⊘

B

Start low-dose aspirin alone and observe

⊘

Begin broad-spectrum antibiotics for presumed temporal cellulitis

⊘

D

✓

E

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

Giant cell arteritis is a granulomatous vasculitis of large and medium arteries, with a preference for the branches of the external carotid. It is a disease of people over 50 and is more common in women. The inflamed temporal artery gives the classic story: a new headache, scalp tenderness when brushing the hair, and jaw claudication, which is jaw pain on chewing from ischemia of the masseter. On exam the temporal artery can be thickened, tender, or pulseless.



It is worth pinning down claudication, since it shows up again in the next few cases: it just means a muscle aches when it has to work because its narrowed artery cannot deliver enough blood, and the ache eases with rest. In the jaw, chewing is the work, so jaw claudication, pain that builds partway through a meal and forces a pause, is a red flag that the arteries feeding the chewing muscles are inflamed and narrowed.



The most critical branch is the blood supply to the optic nerve, the ophthalmic and posterior ciliary arteries. When it occludes, the patient develops anterior ischemic optic neuropathy and can lose vision permanently, sometimes after a warning of transient monocular visual loss (amaurosis fugax), as in this patient. Central retinal artery occlusion and diplopia can also occur. This is an emergency that necessitates urgent treatment.



Giant cell arteritis is associated with polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR). About half of giant cell arteritis patients have PMR, and a smaller fraction of PMR patients go on to develop the arteritis. PMR is characterized by muscular pain and morning stiffness across the shoulder and hip girdles with a high ESR, but the strength is normal, so the problem is pain rather than weakness, which separates it from an inflammatory myopathy, and it responds dramatically to low-dose steroids. Labs in giant cell arteritis show an ESR that is often above 50 and can exceed 100, a raised CRP, and frequently a normocytic anemia and high platelet count.



The temporal artery biopsy shows granulomatous inflammation with multinucleated giant cells, intimal thickening, and fragmentation of the internal elastic lamina. Because the inflammation skips along the vessel, the biopsy needs an adequate length (1 to 2 cm), a negative biopsy does not exclude the disease, and the other side can be sampled if needed. Temporal artery ultrasound can show a 'halo sign,' and MRA or PET can map large-vessel involvement. The treatment point is the one to lock in: start high-dose glucocorticoids the moment you suspect it, before the biopsy, because the biopsy stays positive for one to two weeks on steroids while waiting for biopsy results can lead to permanent loss of vision; intravenous methylprednisolone is typically used. The IL-6 blocker tocilizumab is a steroid-sparing option, low-dose aspirin is often added, and survivors carry a higher long-term risk of thoracic aortic aneurysm, so they need ongoing follow-up.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Delaying steroids until biopsy confirmation risks permanent blindness in the interval. The biopsy matters, but it does not have to come before treatment because a short course of steroids does not erase the histology.

B. Low-dose aspirin may be added as an adjunct, but it does not control the vasculitis or protect vision. Observation alone leaves the optic nerve exposed to infarction.

C. The temporal artery findings come from vasculitis, not a skin or soft-tissue infection, so antibiotics have no role here.

D. The threat is irreversible vision loss from ischemic optic neuropathy, and treatment cannot wait for pathology. High-dose corticosteroids are started as soon as giant cell arteritis is suspected, and the temporal artery biopsy is done shortly after, since it stays positive for one to two weeks on steroids. Treating first is what protects the eye, and this patient already has amaurosis fugax, jaw claudication, a tender temporal artery, and an ESR of 88.

E. Imaging can support large-vessel involvement, but deferring treatment to arrange it repeats the same error as waiting for biopsy. The combination of jaw claudication, amaurosis fugax, a tender temporal artery, and a markedly raised ESR is enough to warrant treatment.

Clinical vignette

A 28-year-old woman presents with 4 months of fatigue, low-grade fevers, and aching in both arms when she carries groceries or works overhead, which eases with rest. She recently felt lightheaded when standing. On examination, the radial pulse is difficult to feel on the left, and the systolic blood pressure differs by 30 mmHg between the two arms. A bruit is audible over the left subclavian region. ESR is elevated. CT angiography shows wall thickening and narrowing of the aortic arch and the origins of the great vessels. Which of the following features best distinguishes this patient's vasculitis from giant cell arteritis?

Answer choices

A

Onset after age 50 with cranial artery involvement and jaw claudication

⊘

B

p-ANCA positivity with pauci-immune glomerulonephritis

⊘

C

Hepatitis B–associated medium-vessel microaneurysms that spare the lungs

⊘

D

Asthma with marked peripheral eosinophilia

⊘

E

Onset before age 40 with involvement of the aorta and its major branches

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Takayasu arteritis is the other large-vessel granulomatous vasculitis, and on a clinical vignette it is almost always a young woman, often of Asian descent, under the age of 40. It attacks the aorta and its main branches, and as the inflamed walls thicken and the lumens narrow, blood flow to the arms and head drops, which is why it earned the name 'pulseless disease.'



It tends to move through two phases. An early systemic phase brings nonspecific fatigue, fever, and weight loss with a raised ESR, and this can delay the diagnosis because nothing yet points to the vessels. A later occlusive phase brings the vascular signs as stenoses set in. The findings are as follows: diminished or absent peripheral pulses, a measurable blood pressure difference between the two arms, bruits over the carotid or subclavian arteries, and limb claudication, the arm-tiring-with-use this patient describes. Narrowing of the vessels to the brain causes dizziness, syncope, or stroke; renal artery involvement drives renovascular hypertension; and dilation of the aortic root can cause aortic regurgitation.



Two physical-exam clues here are worth decoding. A bruit is the whooshing sound a stethoscope picks up when blood has to rush through a narrowed artery, the audio version of a partly pinched hose. And an arm-to-arm blood-pressure gap appears because a narrowed artery on one side delivers less pressure downstream, so the cuff there reads falsely low, which is also why you measure the pressure in the less-affected arm.



Imaging of the aorta with CT or MR angiography (or PET in active disease) shows the wall thickening, stenoses, occlusions, and sometimes aneurysms, and the ESR and CRP track inflammation. Age and location of the diseases is what separates it from giant cell arteritis even though both are granulomatous and both hit large arteries, but giant cell arteritis is a disease of people over 50 with cranial symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, vision loss), while Takayasu is a disease of people under 40 centered on the aortic arch and its branches.



Treatment is corticosteroids first, with steroid-sparing agents (methotrexate, azathioprine, a TNF inhibitor, or tocilizumab) for relapsing disease. Critical fixed stenoses are addressed with angioplasty or bypass, but only once the inflammation is controlled, because operating on an actively inflamed vessel carries poor prognosis. Blood pressure should be measured in the less-affected arm, since a falsely low reading in a stenotic arm can hide dangerous hypertension.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Giant cell arteritis occurs after age 50 with cranial artery involvement, jaw claudication, and the threat of vision loss.

B. This describes a small-vessel ANCA-associated vasculitis such as microscopic polyangiitis, where p-ANCA accompanies a pauci-immune glomerulonephritis. Takayasu is a large-vessel disease and is not defined by ANCA or glomerulonephritis.

C. Polyarteritis nodosa is a medium-vessel necrotizing vasculitis linked to hepatitis B that classically spares the lungs and shows microaneurysms on angiography. It is not a large-vessel disease of the aortic arch.

D.  Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis is a vasculitis, where late-onset asthma and eosinophilia dominate a small-vessel process. That is unrelated to the aortic narrowing seen here.

E. Takayasu arteritis presents before age 40 and involves the aorta and its major branches, producing absent pulses, an inter-arm blood pressure difference, bruits, and limb claudication. That age-plus-lcoation combination is exactly what separates it from giant cell arteritis.

Q15.  Polyarteritis Nodosa

Clinical vignette

A 46-year-old man presents with 6 weeks of weight loss, fevers, abdominal pain that worsens after eating, and new numbness and weakness in the right foot and left hand. His blood pressure is 168/104 mmHg. Examination shows a foot drop on the right, reduced sensation in patches on different limbs, and tender subcutaneous nodules along the forearms with a lacy purplish discoloration of the skin. He was diagnosed with hepatitis B 2 years ago. Chest imaging is clear and he has no respiratory symptoms. ANCA testing is negative. Mesenteric angiography shows multiple small aneurysms studded along medium-sized arteries. Which feature most reliably distinguishes this patient's disease from microscopic polyangiitis?

Answer choices

A

Sparing of the lungs together with negative ANCA serology

✓

B

Pulmonary capillaritis with p-ANCA positivity

⊘

C

Necrotizing granulomas of the upper and lower airways

⊘

D

Late-onset asthma with peripheral eosinophilia

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Polyarteritis nodosa is a necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized arteries. The inflamed walls develop transmural inflammation and fibrinoid necrosis, weaken into microaneurysms, and because new lesions keep forming you see damage at different stages along the same vessel. On a mesenteric or renal angiogram those aneurysms look like beads on a string. 30% of cases are linked to hepatitis B, where circulating viral immune complexes seem to drive the vessel injury, and this patient's known hepatitis B fits that pattern.



The clinical presentation comes from organs losing their medium-vessel blood supply. The kidneys are hit through their arteries rather than their glomeruli, so patients get renovascular hypertension instead of a glomerulonephritis, an important distinction from the ANCA small-vessel diseases. The gut arteries produce postprandial abdominal pain (intestinal angina) and can infarct or bleed. It can also manifest as mononeuritis multiplex, which is a painful condition characterized by isolated damage to peripheral nerves leading to both motor and sensory deficits throughout the body. The skin shows livedo reticularis, tender nodules, ulcers, and even digital gangrene.



The size of the vessel a disease attacks predicts the whole clinical picture, which is the key to the vasculitis chapter. Polyarteritis nodosa goes after medium-sized arteries, the named branches feeding organs, so it strangles whole patches of bowel, nerve, or kidney downstream, giving the gut angina, mononeuritis multiplex, and renovascular high blood pressure seen here. It largely skips the tiniest vessels, the capillaries of the lung and the glomerulus, which is exactly why it spares the lungs and produces a renovascular rather than a glomerular kidney problem, unlike the small-vessel ANCA diseases.



Two negatives findings which are part of the disease are frequently tested; it classically spares the lungs, and it is ANCA-negative. Those two features set it apart from granulomatosis with polyangiitis and microscopic polyangiitis, which involve the lungs and carry ANCA. Diagnosis rests on biopsy of an affected organ (sural nerve, skin, or muscle) showing the transmural necrotizing arteritis, or on the angiographic microaneurysms, and hepatitis B serology should always be checked.



Treatment depends on the trigger. Idiopathic disease is treated with corticosteroids plus cyclophosphamide for severe organ involvement. When hepatitis B serology is positive, the approach shifts toward antiviral therapy with a short course of steroids and sometimes plasma exchange, rather than prolonged immunosuppression that would let the virus replicate. Untreated polyarteritis nodosa has a poor prognosis.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Sparing of the lungs combined with negative ANCA is the most reliable way to separate polyarteritis nodosa from microscopic polyangiitis. The hepatitis B association, renovascular hypertension, mononeuritis multiplex, skin nodules, and angiographic microaneurysms all support PAN, which does not involve the lungs and is ANCA-negative.

B. Microscopic polyangiitis involves the pulmonary capillaries causing alveolar hemorrhage and is p-ANCA is positive. That is the opposite of PAN's lung-sparing, ANCA-negative profile.

C.  C describes granulomatosis with polyangiitis, which produces necrotizing granulomas in the nasopharynx and lungs with c-ANCA. PAN does not form granulomas and does not destroy the airways.

D.  D describes eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, defined by asthma and eosinophilia. PAN has no asthma or eosinophilia component.

E. IgA vasculitis (and IgA nephropathy) is a condition where IgA immune complexes deposit in the mesangium. PAN injures medium arteries and does not deposit mesangial IgA.

Answer: B

Concept explanation

Kawasaki disease is a medium-vessel vasculitis of young children, usually under age 5 and more common in children of Asian descent. Strictly speaking it is idiopathic and behaves more like an autoinflammatory illness than a classic autoantibody disease, but it sits in the medium-vessel vasculitis group because of arteries it affects. The features fit the mnemonic “CRASH and burn”: Conjunctivitis (bilateral, without pus, sparing the limbus), Rash (polymorphous, often with perineal accentuation), Adenopathy (cervical, frequently a single node over 1.5 cm), Strawberry tongue with red cracked lips and oral mucositis, Hands and feet that are swollen and red and later peel around the nails, and the 'burn' of fever lasting at least 5 days.



Without treatment, the inflamed coronary arteries can balloon into aneurysms in around a quarter of children, and those aneurysms can thrombose or rupture, leading to myocardial infarction and sudden death. Treatment cuts that risk to a few percent. Kawasaki disease is a leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries, so an echocardiogram is obtained at diagnosis and repeated during follow-up.



The reason this illness is dangerous is what the inflammation does to the coronary arteries. When the artery wall is inflamed all the way through, it weakens like a worn spot on a balloon and bulges outward into an aneurysm; blood pools and swirls in that pouch, clots can form, and a clot that blocks the coronary artery starves the heart muscle, which is how a preschooler can end up having a heart attack. Cooling the inflammation quickly with IVIG is what keeps the wall from ballooning in the first place.



The labs support the diagnosis without being specific: a raised ESR and CRP, leukocytosis, a high platelet count in the second week, a normocytic anemia, sterile pyuria, and sometimes raised transaminases. The diagnosis is clinical, based on the fever plus the mucocutaneous criteria, and incomplete forms exist that still carry coronary risk, which is why a high index of suspicion is needed in young children with prolonged unexplained fever.



Treatment is intravenous immunoglobulin plus aspirin, and giving it within the first 10 days sharply reduces the risk of coronary aneurysms. Aspirin is used at an anti-inflammatory dose early and then a low antiplatelet dose. Kawasaki disease is one of the few situations where aspirin is given to a child because the coronary benefit outweighs the usual concern about Reye syndrome. Children who do not respond to the first IVIG dose may get a second dose, corticosteroids, or infliximab, and those who develop large (giant) coronary aneurysms are anticoagulated.

B. IVIG plus aspirin given within the first 10 days lowers the risk of coronary artery aneurysms, the dangerous outcome of Kawasaki disease. The fever of at least 5 days with conjunctivitis, mucosal changes, rash, a cervical node, and swollen peeling extremities is the classic presentation that calls for this treatment.

C. Cyclophosphamide is used in severe ANCA-associated and other systemic vasculitides, not in Kawasaki disease, where IVIG and aspirin are standard.

D. Anticoagulation with warfarin is added later for children who already have giant coronary aneurysms, not used as first-line therapy. The initial goal is to abort the inflammation with IVIG and aspirin before aneurysms form.

E. Corticosteroids are reserved for children who do not respond to IVIG or who are high-risk, not used alone up front, and they do not replace IVIG for coronary protection.

Concept explanation

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis is a small-vessel ANCA-associated vasculitis that hits three organs: the upper airway, the lungs, and the kidneys. Its hallmark is a necrotizing granulomatous inflammation combined with vasculitis, and on the kidney the lesion is a pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis, meaning there is little immune-complex deposition to find.



In the nose and sinuses it causes chronic crusting, recurrent epistaxis, and destruction that can perforate the septum and collapse the bridge into a saddle-nose deformity, along with otitis, hearing loss, and sometimes subglottic stenosis or an orbital mass. In the lungs it produces nodules that often cavitate, and it can cause alveolar hemorrhage and hemoptysis. In the kidneys it causes a rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis that will scar if it is not treated quickly. Together this upper-airway, lower-airway, and renal triad is the pattern to recognize.



Granulomatosis with polyangiitis is the c-ANCA disease, with antibodies against proteinase 3 (anti-PR3), giving a cytoplasmic staining pattern that correlates strongly with this diagnosis, especially when the upper airway is involved. A minority of cases are MPO-positive instead, so ANCA results are correlated with the clinical picture and the biopsy rather than treated as absolute.



ANCA stands for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, and it is exactly that: an antibody aimed at proteins packed inside neutrophils, the body’s first-responder white cells. Two targets matter. Antibodies against proteinase-3 light up the whole cytoplasm under the microscope, the cytoplasmic or c-ANCA pattern, and track with this disease. Antibodies against myeloperoxidase ring the nucleus instead, the perinuclear or p-ANCA pattern, seen more in the other two ANCA vasculitides. The antibodies do not merely mark the disease; they actually switch the neutrophils on, which is what injures the small vessels.



On biopsy necrotizing granulomatous vasculitis is seen, and the renal biopsy shows the pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis (scant staining on immunofluorescence, unlike the 'full house' of lupus or the linear IgG of anti-GBM disease). The upper-airway destruction is what most reliably separates it from microscopic polyangiitis, which has neither granulomas nor nasopharyngeal disease. Treatment is induction with corticosteroids plus rituximab or cyclophosphamide, then maintenance with rituximab or azathioprine, with TMP-SMX used in limited disease and for Pneumocystis prophylaxis. It tends to relapse, and pulmonary hemorrhage with progressive renal failure prompts urgent treatment.

B. Systemic lupus erythematosus, whose nephritis shows abundant immune-complex ('full house') staining, which is the opposite of pauci-immune. Lupus does not cause cavitary lung nodules or a saddle-nose deformity.

C. The upper-airway destruction, cavitary lung nodules, and pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis are the classic triad of granulomatosis with polyangiitis, which is the c-ANCA/anti-PR3 disease. That serology fits the presentation best.

D. IgA immune complexes deposit in vessels and the glomerular mesangium in IgA vasculitis. This is a different small-vessel disease and does not produce destructive upper-airway granulomas.

E.  This describes anti-GBM (Goodpasture) disease, which causes pulmonary hemorrhage and glomerulonephritis with linear IgG along the basement membrane rather than pauci-immune disease, and it does not destroy the nasal septum.

Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Picture an immune system that keeps lighting small fires in the linings of the body, with no single antibody or organ to blame. That is Behçet disease, an unusual vasculitis that can hit blood vessels of any size, vein or artery, which is why it is grouped under "variable-vessel" vasculitis. It clusters along the old Silk Road, so the classic patient comes from Turkey, the Middle East, or East Asia, and it carries a strong tie to HLA-B51. There is no defining autoantibody, which is part of what makes it stand out from the diseases around it.



The characteristic features of this disease are recurrent ulcers and eye inflammation. Painful oral aphthous ulcers are almost universal and usually come first, recurring every few weeks. Genital ulcers follow a similar pattern and tend to scar. In the eye, a relapsing uveitis can be seen. It can involve the front of the eye (anterior uveitis, sometimes with a hypopyon, a visible layer of pus in the anterior chamber) or the back (posterior uveitis and retinal vasculitis), and untreated it is can lead to blindness. Skin findings typically include erythema nodosum and acne-like or pustular lesions.



One bedside test is worth knowing. The pathergy reaction is an exaggerated skin response to minor trauma: prick the skin with a sterile needle and a small pustule develops at the site over the next day or two. It reflects the neutrophilic inflammation of the disease and supports the diagnosis, though it is more common in patients from endemic regions. Beyond skin, eyes, and mucosa, Behçet can cause venous thrombosis, arterial aneurysms (pulmonary artery aneurysms are a feared complication), a usually mild arthritis, gut ulcers that resemble inflammatory bowel disease, and central nervous system involvement.



The pathergy test makes sense once you know the engine of Behçet is an overeager neutrophil. In most people a clean needle prick heals quietly, but here the neutrophils are primed to swarm at the slightest provocation, so even sterile trauma draws a crowd and raises a small pus-filled bump within a day or two. The same trigger-happy neutrophils explain the sterile pustules, the gut ulcers, and the hypopyon, that visible layer of settled white cells in the front of the eye.



There is no single confirmatory blood test, so the diagnosis is clinical, built on recurrent oral ulceration plus some combination of genital ulcers, eye disease, skin lesions, and a positive pathergy test. Treatment is matched to severity: colchicine and topical steroids help mucocutaneous disease, while sight-threatening eye disease, major vessel involvement, or neurologic disease calls for systemic corticosteroids plus immunosuppressants such as azathioprine, cyclosporine, or a TNF inhibitor, and apremilast is now used for the oral ulcers. The thing to recognize on a test is the young patient from an endemic region with recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and a pathergy reaction, without a disease-defining antibody.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Anti-CCP is the most specific antibody for rheumatoid arthritis and predicts erosive joint disease. Behçet can cause a mild arthritis, but it is not driven by anti-CCP, and the ulcers, uveitis, and pathergy point elsewhere.

B. Anti-dsDNA is specific for systemic lupus erythematosus and tracks with lupus nephritis. Lupus can cause oral ulcers, but they are usually painless, and it does not produce the genital ulcers, hypopyon uveitis, and pathergy of Behçet.

C. The pathergy reaction, a sterile pustule appearing at a site of minor skin trauma like a needle stick, reflects the exaggerated neutrophilic inflammation of Behçet disease and supports the diagnosis. In a young man from an endemic region with recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis with a hypopyon, and erythema nodosum, it fits the clinical picture, since Behçet has no single defining autoantibody.

D. The genetic tie in Behçet is to HLA-B51, not HLA-B27. HLA-B27 belongs to the seronegative spondyloarthropathies (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, and the like), which present with inflammatory back pain and sacroiliitis rather than recurrent mucocutaneous ulcers and uveitis with a hypopyon.

E. C-ANCA against proteinase 3 is for granulomatosis with polyangiitis, which destroys the upper airway, cavitates the lungs, and causes a pauci-immune glomerulonephritis. That is a different vasculitis and does not produce recurrent oral and genital ulcers with a hypopyon.

Q22.  Hashimoto Thyroiditis

Clinical vignette

A 41-year-old woman presents with 8 months of progressive fatigue, weight gain, despite no dietary changes, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, and thinning hair. She also reports menstrual irregularity with heavier bleeding. Examination shows bradycardia (HR 54/min), delayed relaxation phase of deep tendon reflexes, periorbital puffiness, and a firm, mildly tender, diffusely enlarged thyroid gland. Laboratory studies: TSH 48 mIU/L (markedly elevated, normal 0.4-4.0), free T4 0.4 ng/dL (low, normal 0.8-1.8), anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibody 1,200 IU/mL (markedly elevated, normal <35), anti-thyroglobulin antibody also positive. She is started on levothyroxine. Fine-needle aspiration of the thyroid would most likely show which of the following histologic findings?

Answer choices

A

Diffuse hyperplasia with tall columnar follicular cells and scalloped colloid

⊘

B

Multinucleated giant cells surrounding damaged follicles with granulomatous inflammation

⊘

C

Dense fibrous tissue replacing thyroid parenchyma with sparse cellularity

⊘

D

Lymphocytic infiltrate with germinal centers and Hürthle cell metaplasia of follicular epithelium

✓

E

Sheets of atypical cells with Orphan Annie eye nuclei and psammoma bodies

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

In Hashimoto thyroiditis, immune cells decide that the thyroid hormone producing “thyroid factory” is enemy territory and decide to infiltrate in. They build little command posts called germinal centers right on the factory floor, where B cells multiply and make antibodies against thyroid proteins. The workers under attack, the follicular epithelial cells, change shape and swell with pink, mitochondria-packed cytoplasm, becoming the Hürthle cells (also called oncocytes or Askanazy cells) that pathologists look for. Over months to years the immune attack wrecks enough machinery that hormone output fails and the patient becomes hypothyroid. This is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient countries.



A quick word on the Hürthle cell, since it is the histologic tell. When a thyroid follicular cell is under chronic immune stress it crams itself with extra mitochondria to keep up, and all those mitochondria turn the cytoplasm bulky and bright pink under the microscope. That oncocytic change, sitting alongside lymphocytes that have built germinal centers, is the gland being colonized and worn down by the immune system from the inside.



The damage is both cellular and antibody-driven. Th1 cells release IFN-gamma and TNF-alpha and recruit cytotoxic CD8+ T cells that kill thyrocytes directly. At the same time, B cells make antibodies against two thyroid proteins. Anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) targets the enzyme that attaches iodine to tyrosine during hormone synthesis, is present in roughly 90% of patients, and is the most sensitive marker. Anti-thyroglobulin targets the storage protein in colloid and is less specific but commonly positive. Both can fix complement and add to follicular injury. Early on, the destruction can dump pre-formed hormone into the blood and cause a brief hyperthyroid phase ("Hashitoxicosis") with palpitations and weight loss before the slide into hypothyroidism, which is why some patients first show up with hyperthyroid symptoms even though the underlying process is destructive.



Hashimoto rarely travels alone. It sits in the autoimmune polyglandular spectrum, so patients carry a higher risk of other organ-specific autoimmune diseases: type 1 diabetes, Addison disease, pernicious anemia, vitiligo, alopecia areata, celiac disease, and primary ovarian insufficiency. This is reflected by the shared HLA susceptibility (HLA-DR3, HLA-DR5) and a broader failure of tolerance to self. Autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 2, the adult-onset form, pairs Addison disease with autoimmune thyroid disease, with or without type 1 diabetes. Because of this, it is reasonable to screen Hashimoto patients periodically with low-cost tests such as fasting glucose, vitamin B12, and anti-tissue transglutaminase.



One uncommon but high-yield association is primary thyroid lymphoma (usually a diffuse large B-cell or MALT lymphoma), with a risk many times that of the general population, rare in absolute terms but signaled by rapid thyroid enlargement in a patient with long-standing Hashimoto. Treatment of the hypothyroidism is straightforward; levothyroxine, dosed to bring the TSH into range (around 1 to 2.5 mIU/L for most patients) is administered orally to the patient and TSH levels are rechecked every 4 to 8 weeks until stable and then yearly.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. This describes Graves disease. The TSH-receptor-stimulating antibody drives diffuse follicular hyperplasia with tall columnar cells and scalloped colloid where the epithelium resorbs it at the edges. Graves causes hyperthyroidism rather than hypothyroidism, and the antibody is against the TSH receptor, not TPO and thyroglobulin.

B. Subacute granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis is a post-viral, self-limited inflammation. Patients have a painful, tender thyroid (often after an upper respiratory infection), a high ESR, and a transient hyperthyroid phase followed by transient hypothyroidism and recovery, with giant cells and granulomas around damaged follicles rather than the lymphocytic-with-germinal-centers pattern of Hashimoto.

C. Riedel thyroiditis is a rare disease in which dense fibrous tissue replaces the gland and can extend into surrounding structures, giving a rock-hard thyroid that mimics malignancy and is now considered part of the IgG4-related disease spectrum. The biopsy shows fibrosis with sparse cellularity, not the dense lymphocytic infiltrate of Hashimoto.

D. The histologic signature of Hashimoto thyroiditis is a dense lymphocytic infiltrate with germinal centers forming inside the thyroid, essentially a tertiary lymphoid organ growing in the gland, alongside surviving follicles lined by Hürthle cells (large polygonal cells with abundant pink granular cytoplasm packed with mitochondria). With the clinical and serologic picture, these findings are diagnostic.

E. Papillary thyroid carcinoma is the most common thyroid cancer, with "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei and psammoma bodies. It presents as a discrete cold nodule rather than diffuse enlargement, and the biopsy shows malignant epithelial cells, not a lymphocytic infiltrate. Hashimoto carries a small increased risk of papillary cancer, but the histology in this stem reflects the autoimmune destruction itself.

References: Caturegli, P., De Remigis, A., & Rose, N. R. (2014). Hashimoto thyroiditis: Clinical and diagnostic criteria. Autoimmunity Reviews, 13(4–5), 391–397.

Q23.  Graves Disease

Clinical vignette

A 32-year-old woman presents with 4 months of progressive weight loss (12 lb) despite increased appetite, palpitations, heat intolerance, anxiety, and tremulousness. She also reports prominent eye discomfort with redness, tearing, and double vision when looking up and to the side. Examination reveals a thin woman with HR 118/min and BP 142/74 mmHg, lid lag, proptosis (exophthalmometer reading 24 mm bilaterally), restricted upgaze, a fine resting tremor, warm moist skin, and a diffusely enlarged, smooth, non-tender thyroid with an audible bruit. Pretibial skin shows non-pitting waxy plaques. Laboratory studies: TSH <0.01 mIU/L (suppressed), free T4 4.2 ng/dL (elevated), free T3 9.8 pg/mL (elevated), thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) positive at 380% (normal <140%). Radioactive iodine uptake is diffusely elevated. Which of the following best explains why this patient developed hyperthyroidism rather than hypothyroidism, in contrast to a patient with anti-TPO antibodies and Hashimoto thyroiditis?

Answer choices

A

The autoantibody fixes complement and destroys thyroid follicular cells

⊘

B

The autoantibody blocks iodine uptake by the sodium-iodide symporter

⊘

C

The autoantibody binds intracellular thyroglobulin

⊘

D

Th1 cytotoxic T cells release pre-formed thyroid hormone from damaged follicles

~

E

The autoantibody binds and activates the TSH receptor, mimicking TSH and continuously stimulating hormone production

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Picture the thyroid factory with a manager who controls a "make more hormone" switch. Normally the brain flips that switch by sending TSH when the body needs more thyroid hormone, and the switch is a receptor on the surface of every follicular cell. In Graves disease, the body makes an antibody that disguises itself as the brain's signal. This thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (also called TSH-receptor antibody) sits on the TSH receptor and turns it on, and unlike real TSH, which the brain can dial back through negative feedback, the antibody keeps the receptor switched on. The factory now runs at full capacity with no off switch, and the result is hyperthyroidism. This is a type II hypersensitivity reaction, but a stimulating one rather than a destructive one.



It is worth pausing on the unusual trick here, an antibody acting as an agonist. Most antibodies either tag a cell for destruction or block a receptor, but the Graves antibody docks onto the TSH receptor and switches it on, behaving exactly like the hormone it imitates. Because antibodies are not part of the brain's feedback loop, nothing tells it to stop, so the gland is held in the on position indefinitely, which is the whole reason the patient runs hot instead of cold.



The hyperthyroid state itself produces the familiar picture: weight loss despite a good appetite, heat intolerance, palpitations and a fast pulse, tremor, anxiety, warm moist skin, and a diffusely enlarged gland that can have a bruit from increased blood flow. Because the whole gland is being driven, radioactive iodine uptake is high and diffuse, which separates Graves from other causes of hyperthyroidism where uptake is low (a destructive thyroiditis dumping stored hormone, or exogenous hormone intake). 



Two findings are specific to Graves and are not explained by thyroid hormone excess alone, because they come from the antibody and the inflammation it drives outside the thyroid. Graves ophthalmopathy is the proptosis, lid retraction, and restricted eye movements seen here, caused by activated fibroblasts behind the eye that take up the same TSH receptor as a target, recruit inflammation, and lay down glycosaminoglycans that swell the extraocular muscles and orbital fat. Pretibial myxedema is the waxy, non-pitting plaque over the shins from a similar process in the skin. Smoking worsens the eye disease, so cessation is recommended.



Diagnosis rests on a suppressed TSH with high free T4 and T3, a positive TSH-receptor antibody, and diffusely elevated radioiodine uptake. Treatment has three options: antithyroid drugs (methimazole, or propylthiouracil in the first trimester of pregnancy and in thyroid storm), radioactive iodine ablation, or surgery, with beta-blockers for symptom control along the way. Each definitive option tends to leave the patient hypothyroid afterward, which is then treated with levothyroxine. Orbital disease is managed separately, and severe cases may need steroids, the IGF-1-receptor antibody teprotumumab, or orbital decompression.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. This option describes the destructive, complement-fixing antibody mechanism more typical of Hashimoto and would lead to hypothyroidism, not the sustained hyperthyroidism seen here. The Graves antibody stimulates rather than destroys.

B. This is wrong: a blocking antibody against iodine uptake would reduce hormone production and cause hypothyroidism. The Graves antibody activates the receptor; it does not block iodine transport.

C. Anti-thyroglobulin binds the storage protein in colloid and is a Hashimoto-associated marker, and even there the disease comes from cytotoxic and antibody-mediated destruction rather than from interfering with synthesis. The Graves antibody acts on the surface TSH receptor, not on intracellular thyroglobulin.

D. Hashitoxicosis is a brief hyperthyroid phase in early Hashimoto when destruction releases stored hormone. That is self-limited, leads on to hypothyroidism, and shows LOW radioiodine uptake because the gland is being destroyed rather than stimulated. This patient has sustained hyperthyroidism with high diffuse uptake and orbital and pretibial findings, which point to ongoing stimulation.

E. In Graves disease the antibody binds and activates the TSH receptor, mimicking TSH and driving continuous hormone production. Because it is not subject to the negative feedback that shuts off real TSH, the gland keeps making hormone, which is why the patient is hyperthyroid with diffusely elevated radioiodine uptake, the opposite of the destructive antibody process in Hashimoto.

Q24.  Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus

Clinical vignette

An 11-year-old previously healthy girl is brought to the emergency department with 2 days of vomiting, abdominal pain, and progressive lethargy. Her mother reports the patient had been drinking large amounts of water and urinating frequently for the past 3 weeks, along with unintentional weight loss of 8 lb. On examination, she is somnolent but arousable. Vital signs: HR 124/min, BP 102/64 mmHg, RR 28/min (deep and labored, with fruity breath odor), capillary refill 3 seconds. Laboratory studies: glucose 542 mg/dL, sodium 132 mEq/L, potassium 5.4 mEq/L, bicarbonate 8 mEq/L, anion gap 24, blood pH 7.18, urine ketones 4+, hemoglobin A1c 12.4%. After stabilization with IV fluids and insulin, autoantibody panel returns positive for anti-GAD65, anti-IA-2 (islet antigen 2), and anti-insulin antibodies. Pancreatic biopsy is not performed but if obtained would most likely show which of the following histologic findings?

Answer choices

A

Insulitis; lymphocytic infiltrate of pancreatic islets with predominant CD8+ T cells destroying beta cells

✓

B

Amyloid deposition within islets and beta-cell exhaustion without significant inflammation

⊘

C

Acinar atrophy and ductal dilation with calcifications and fibrosis

⊘

D

Periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate with storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis

⊘

E

Sheets of small uniform cells with salt-and-pepper chromatin invading surrounding tissue

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Picture the pancreatic islets as small power plants scattered through the pancreas, each one making insulin to keep blood sugar in check. In type 1 diabetes, T cells decide these plants are enemy installations and take them out one by one. The attack is slow and methodical, running over months to years, and by the time symptoms appear (thirst, frequent urination, weight loss, sometimes diabetic ketoacidosis as in this child) about 80 to 90% of the beta cells are already gone. This is why type 1 diabetes seems to arrive "suddenly" even though it began years earlier.



The destruction is mainly Th1-driven and carried out by CD8+ T cells. Autoreactive CD4+ Th1 cells specific for islet antigens activate macrophages and recruit cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, which kill beta cells through perforin and granzyme and the Fas pathway. The biopsy hallmark is insulitis: a lymphocytic infiltrate in and around the islets, with CD8+ T cells predominating and selectively destroying beta cells while sparing the alpha, delta, and PP cells in the same islet. That selectivity is the giveaway that the attack is antigen-driven rather than indiscriminate. Several autoantibodies appear in the blood, often years before symptoms: anti-GAD65 (glutamic acid decarboxylase), anti-IA-2 (islet antigen 2), anti-insulin, and anti-ZnT8 (zinc transporter 8). These antibodies do not themselves kill beta cells, since the destruction is T-cell mediated; they are markers of the underlying process, and the number of positive antibodies predicts progression (one antibody carries a modest lifetime risk, while two or more carry roughly a 50 to 70% risk over 5 years).



How does a T cell actually kill a beta cell? A cytotoxic CD8 cell recognizes a fragment of beta-cell protein displayed on the cell surface, then fires perforin to punch pores in the target membrane and pushes granzymes through them to trip the cell's self-destruct program. It does this only to cells flashing the offending islet antigen, which is why the beta cells disappear while their alpha and delta neighbors in the very same islet are left untouched.





Genetic risk is dominated by HLA. About 90% of patients carry HLA-DR3, HLA-DR4, or both, and the DR3/DR4 heterozygote is at highest risk because the two alleles present islet peptides synergistically. Non-HLA genes (INS, PTPN22, CTLA4, IL2RA) add smaller contributions. The disease is now staged: stage 1 is asymptomatic with multiple autoantibodies and normal glucose, stage 2 adds dysglycemia while still asymptomatic, and stage 3 is overt diabetes with symptoms. Mapping this timeline opened a window to intervene before symptoms; teplizumab, a humanized anti-CD3 antibody, was approved in 2022 for stage 2 disease and delays progression to stage 3 by about 2 to 3 years.



Treatment of established type 1 diabetes is lifelong insulin. The honeymoon period after diagnosis, a few weeks to months of reduced insulin need, reflects a brief recovery of the surviving beta cells once glucose toxicity eases, but those cells are eventually lost and the patient becomes fully insulin-dependent. As with Hashimoto, type 1 diabetes clusters with celiac disease, autoimmune thyroid disease, Addison disease, and pernicious anemia in the autoimmune polyendocrine family, hence appropriate screening is recommended.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Insulitis is the histologic hallmark of type 1 diabetes: a lymphocytic infiltrate in and around the islets with CD8+ T cells predominating and selectively killing beta cells while sparing the other islet cell types. That selectivity reflects an antigen-driven attack against beta-cell antigens such as insulin, GAD65, and IA-2. An 11-year-old with new-onset DKA, weight loss, multiple islet autoantibodies, and a high HbA1c is a classic presentation.

B. In type 2 diabetes, islet amyloid polypeptide deposits in the islets and beta cells exhaust with little inflammation, in the setting of insulin resistance and obesity. A lean 11-year-old with rapid weight loss, DKA, and positive islet autoantibodies does not fit type 2 diabetes, which lacks autoantibodies and rarely presents with DKA.

C. Chronic pancreatitis is marked by acinar atrophy, ductal dilation, calcifications, and fibrosis, causing exocrine insufficiency and eventually a secondary diabetes. Those patients have abdominal pain and malabsorption over a long course, not the acute autoimmune insulitis seen here.

D. Type 1 autoimmune (IgG4-related) pancreatitis contains a periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, storiform fibrosis, and obliterative phlebitis, typically in an older adult with painless jaundice and a sausage-shaped pancreas. That is unrelated to this child's presentation.

E. This option describes a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, where the salt-and-pepper chromatin is characteristic. These are neoplasms, not autoimmune disease, and do not cause DKA or islet autoantibodies; an insulinoma would actually cause hypoglycemia, the opposite of this patient's hyperglycemia.

Q25.  Addison Disease (Autoimmune Adrenalitis)

Clinical vignette

A 38-year-old woman presents with 6 months of fatigue, light-headedness on standing, and a 9 kg weight loss. She craves salty foods and has noticed her skin darkening, especially in the palmar creases, over old scars, and along the gums. Vital signs show a blood pressure of 96/60 mmHg lying down that falls to 80/52 mmHg on standing. Laboratory studies: sodium 128 mEq/L, potassium 5.6 mEq/L, glucose 62 mg/dL, with mild eosinophilia. An 8 a.m. serum cortisol is low and the plasma ACTH is markedly elevated. A cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test fails to raise the cortisol. Anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies are positive. Which of the following findings best distinguishes this patient's primary adrenal insufficiency from secondary (pituitary) adrenal insufficiency?

Answer choices

A

Hypertension and hypokalemia from aldosterone excess

⊘

B

Hyperpigmentation and hyperkalemia, from loss of both cortisol and aldosterone with a compensatory rise in ACTH

✓

C

Elevated cortisol with central obesity and purple striae

⊘

D

High aldosterone with suppressed renin

⊘

E

Low ACTH with preserved aldosterone and pale skin

⊘

Answer: B

Concept explanation

The adrenal cortex of the adrenal gland is a three-layer gland sitting on top of the kidney, making aldosterone in its outer layer, cortisol in the middle, and androgens deep down. In autoimmune Addison disease, the immune system destroys that cortex, with antibodies against 21-hydroxylase (an enzyme in the cortisol and aldosterone pathways) marking the process. Lose the cortex and you lose both cortisol and aldosterone, which is what separates this primary problem from a pituitary problem, where only the ACTH-driven cortisol arm is affected and aldosterone, run by the renin-angiotensin system, keeps going. 

In the developed world autoimmune destruction is the leading cause of Addison disease; worldwide, tuberculosis has historically been a major cause. 



Losing cortisol leads to fatigue, poor appetite, weight loss, nausea, low blood sugar, and a blunted stress response, and it removes cortisol's permissive support of vascular tone, which contributes to low blood pressure. Losing aldosterone is what gives the disease its signature electrolyte pattern: without aldosterone the kidney wastes sodium and retains potassium, so the patient develops hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, salt craving, and orthostatic hypotension from volume loss. A mild eosinophilia and lymphocytosis can appear because cortisol normally restrains those cells.



Aldosterone's day job explains the lab panel. In the distal kidney it tells the tubule to reabsorb sodium, dragging water back to prop up blood pressure, and to dump potassium and acid into the urine in exchange. Strip aldosterone away and that trade collapses: sodium and water leak out (the low sodium, the orthostatic drop in pressure, the salt craving) while potassium and acid pile up (the hyperkalemia and mild acidosis). That electrolyte fingerprint is what tells you the outer adrenal layer is gone, not just the cortisol arm.



As cortisol falls, the pituitary ramps up ACTH to try to whip the failing adrenal back into action. ACTH is cut from a larger precursor, pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), the same precursor that yields melanocyte-stimulating hormone, so high ACTH output comes with extra melanocyte stimulation and the skin darkens, most visibly in the palmar creases, over scars, on the gums, and in sun-exposed areas. This hyperpigmentation only happens when ACTH is high, which means it points to a primary adrenal problem; in secondary insufficiency the ACTH is low and the skin stays pale.



Diagnosis combines a low morning cortisol with a high ACTH and a cosyntropin stimulation test that fails to raise cortisol, with positive 21-hydroxylase antibodies confirming the autoimmune cause. Treatment replaces both hormones: a glucocorticoid (hydrocortisone or prednisone) plus a mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone), with patients taught to take stress doses during illness and to carry injectable hydrocortisone. Adrenal crisis is an emergency manifesting as hypotension, vomiting, and hyponatremia, often triggered by an infection or a missed dose, treated immediately with IV hydrocortisone and saline. Autoimmune Addison disease frequently keeps company with autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes as part of autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 2.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome) causes hypertension and hypokalemia from aldosterone excess. That is the mirror image of Addison disease, where aldosterone is deficient.

B. In primary adrenal insufficiency the whole cortex is destroyed, so both cortisol and aldosterone are lost. Aldosterone loss gives hyperkalemia (along with hyponatremia and salt craving), and the low cortisol drives a compensatory rise in ACTH, whose shared POMC origin with melanocyte-stimulating hormone produces hyperpigmentation. Those two findings together point to a primary adrenal problem rather than a pituitary one.

C. Cushing syndrome is essentially cortisol excess, central obesity, and purple striae. This patient has cortisol deficiency, not excess.

D. This again describes primary hyperaldosteronism, with high aldosterone and suppressed renin. Addison disease has low aldosterone with high renin, the reverse pattern.

E. In secondary (pituitary) adrenal insufficiency, ACTH is low, so there is no hyperpigmentation, and aldosterone is preserved because it runs off renin-angiotensin rather than ACTH, so potassium stays normal. That is the opposite of this patient's high ACTH and hyperkalemia.

Q26.  Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndrome Type 2

Clinical vignette

A 34-year-old woman with a 4-year history of type 1 diabetes presents with worsening fatigue, weight loss, and lightheadedness. Two years ago she was diagnosed with autoimmune hypothyroidism and started on levothyroxine. On examination her blood pressure is 92/58 mmHg with hyperpigmentation of the palmar creases and buccal mucosa. Laboratory studies show hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, a low morning cortisol with a high ACTH, and positive anti-21-hydroxylase antibodies, confirming new autoimmune adrenal insufficiency. Her parathyroid function is normal and she has no history of recurrent fungal infections. Which of the following best characterizes her syndrome, in contrast to autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1?

Answer choices

A

A childhood AIRE mutation causing chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, and Addison disease

⊘

B

An isolated single-gene defect in the TSH receptor

⊘

C

An adult-onset, polygenic clustering of Addison disease with autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes, tied to HLA-DR3/DR4

✓

D

A paraneoplastic syndrome arising from a thymoma

⊘

E

A FOXP3 mutation eliminating regulatory T cells, with early enteropathy and dermatitis

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Think of organ-specific autoimmunity as something that tends to come in packages rather than one disease at a time. When a person loses tolerance to one endocrine gland, they are more likely to lose it to others, hence the name, autoimmune polyendocrine syndromes. Type 2 is the common, adult-onset one, and it is polygenic, built on shared HLA susceptibility (HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4) rather than a single gene.



The defining members of type 2 are Addison disease plus autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto or Graves), with or without type 1 diabetes. The combination of Addison disease and autoimmune thyroid disease is sometimes called Schmidt syndrome. Other organ-specific autoimmune conditions travel with it as well, including celiac disease, pernicious anemia, vitiligo, and myasthenia gravis. This patient shows the pattern cleanly: type 1 diabetes, then autoimmune hypothyroidism, and now autoimmune Addison disease.



In contrast, type 1, also called APECED, is frequently tested. Type 1 is a childhood disease caused by mutations in a single gene, AIRE, which normally lets the thymus display self-antigens so autoreactive T cells can be deleted. Without AIRE, tolerance fails broadly, and the classic triad is chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, and Addison disease, often with the candidiasis appearing first in early childhood. The single-gene, autosomal recessive inheritance and the presence of candidiasis and hypoparathyroidism set type 1 apart from the polygenic, adult-onset, thyroid-and-diabetes-flavored type 2. This patient has normal parathyroid function and no candidiasis, which argues against type 1.



The wrong single-gene answers are worth sorting by where tolerance breaks. AIRE works in the thymus, parading self-proteins so that any T cell reacting to them is deleted before it graduates; lose AIRE and self-reactive cells escape, which is type 1 (APECED). FOXP3 builds regulatory T cells, the patrol that restrains self-reactive cells out in the body; lose it and you get IPEX. Type 2 has neither gene broken, it is many small risk variants, chiefly HLA-DR3 and DR4, stacking up in an adult, which is why it clusters glands over years rather than striking in infancy.



Management is to treat each component and to stay alert for the next one to appear. Replacing thyroid hormone in a patient who also has undiagnosed Addison disease can precipitate an adrenal crisis by speeding cortisol metabolism, so when both are present the adrenal insufficiency is treated first or together. Patients are followed over time with periodic screening for the other diseases in the cluster, since they can surface years apart, exactly as they did here.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Type 1 (APECED) is caused by an AIRE mutation and presenting in childhood with chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism, and Addison disease. This patient has normal parathyroids and no candidiasis, so it is not type 1.

B. A single-gene TSH-receptor defect would cause an isolated thyroid problem, not a multi-gland syndrome that also includes Addison disease and type 1 diabetes.

C. Autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 2 is the adult-onset, polygenic cluster tied to HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4, defined by Addison disease with autoimmune thyroid disease, with or without type 1 diabetes. This patient has all three components and lacks the candidiasis and hypoparathyroidism of type 1.

D. A thymoma can drive paraneoplastic autoimmunity such as myasthenia gravis or pure red cell aplasia, but it does not explain this patient's combination of Addison disease, autoimmune thyroid disease, and type 1 diabetes, which is the polyendocrine type 2 pattern.

E. IPEX syndrome arises from a FOXP3 mutation that eliminates regulatory T cells, presenting in infancy with severe enteropathy, dermatitis, and endocrinopathy. That is a different, X-linked disease, not the adult endocrine clustering seen here.

Q27.  Celiac Disease

Clinical vignette

A 32-year-old woman presents with 8 months of chronic intermittent diarrhea, 14-pound weight loss, abdominal bloating, and progressive fatigue. She also reports a recurrent intensely pruritic vesicular rash on her elbows and knees. Her past medical history is significant for type 1 diabetes mellitus diagnosed at age 14. Examination shows pallor, angular cheilitis, and grouped tense vesicles with excoriations on the extensor surfaces of both elbows. Laboratory studies: hemoglobin 9.4 g/dL, MCV 76 fL, ferritin 8 ng/mL, total IgA normal, anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA antibodies strongly positive (185 U/mL; normal <20), anti-endomysial IgA antibodies positive. Genetic testing reveals HLA-DQ2 positive. Duodenal biopsy shows total villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes. Which of the following best explains the molecular mechanism by which dietary gluten triggers the immune response in this patient?



Why flattening the villi makes someone sick: the small intestine absorbs nutrients across millions of finger-like villi that multiply its surface area enormously, like the deep pile of a towel versus a flat sheet. The immune attack shears those fingers down to a smooth surface, so iron, folate, calcium, and the fat-soluble vitamins sail past unabsorbed, which is why the headline finding in many adults is a stubborn iron-deficiency anemia rather than diarrhea.

Answer choices

A

Gliadin acts as a superantigen, cross-linking T-cell receptors and MHC class II nonspecifically

⊘

B

Gliadin is recognized directly by IgE on mast cells, causing immediate hypersensitivity in the gut

⊘

C

Gliadin binds Fc receptors on plasma cells, triggering polyclonal IgA secretion

⊘

D

Tissue transglutaminase deamidates gliadin peptides, which then bind HLA-DQ2/DQ8 with high affinity and activate gluten-specific CD4+ T cells

✓

E

Bacterial lipopolysaccharide activates Toll-like receptor 4 on intestinal epithelial cells

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

The immune system keeps a compressed list of enemies that it has encountered in its memory, each one a small piece of protein that, when an MHC molecule shows it to a T cell, triggers an attack. Most foods you eat never make it into this list because their peptides do not fit any MHC molecule well. Gluten, and specifically the wheat protein gliadin, is an exception. On its own, gliadin does not fit either, but an enzyme in the gut called tissue transglutaminase modifies it by converting certain glutamine residues into glutamic acid, a step called deamidation. The deamidated gliadin picks up negative charges that slot neatly into the positively charged grooves of two specific MHC class II molecules, HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8. About 95% of celiac patients carry HLA-DQ2 and most of the rest carry HLA-DQ8, and people with neither essentially cannot get the disease.



Once deamidated gliadin is presented on DQ2 or DQ8, gluten-specific CD4+ T cells switch on and release IFN-gamma in a Th1-dominant response. They help B cells make the antibodies that the lab uses: anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (the main test, with sensitivity and specificity both above 95% as long as the patient is still eating gluten), anti-endomysial IgA (highly specific), and anti-deamidated gliadin peptide antibodies. There is a testing pearl here: a few percent of celiac patients have selective IgA deficiency, which would give a false-negative anti-tTG IgA, so you check a total IgA at the same time and switch to IgG-based tests if it is low. The activated T cells also recruit cytotoxic CD8+ T cells that kill the intestinal lining, producing the biopsy triad of villous atrophy (the absorptive finger-like projections flatten), crypt hyperplasia (the deeper glands proliferate to compensate), and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes.



The presentations are varied, which is why celiac is missed so often. The classic form is chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea, weight loss, and bloating. But the most common single presentation in adults is iron deficiency anemia, and the disease can also show up as osteoporosis, deficiencies of B12, folate, and fat-soluble vitamins, raised transaminases, infertility, or, in children, failure to thrive and short stature. The skin form is dermatitis herpetiformis, the intensely itchy grouped vesicles on the extensor surfaces in this patient, caused by IgA deposits in the dermal papillae and treated with dapsone plus a gluten-free diet.



Roughly 5 to 10% of people with type 1 diabetes have coexisting celiac, and autoimmune thyroid disease and Addison disease are over-represented as well, which fits this patient's existing type 1 diabetes. There are also ties to Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, and selective IgA deficiency. Treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. Left untreated, the long-term risks include enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, small bowel adenocarcinoma, refractory disease, and metabolic bone disease.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Superantigens bind MHC II and T-cell receptor beta chains nonspecifically and activate a large fraction of all T cells regardless of specificity, as in toxic shock syndrome. Celiac is the opposite: highly antigen-specific for gluten, highly HLA-restricted, and dependent on tTG-mediated deamidation.

B. IgE-mediated wheat allergy is a different disease that causes immediate symptoms like urticaria or anaphylaxis within minutes. Celiac develops over days to weeks of gluten exposure, and the anti-tTG positivity and villous atrophy rule out an immediate IgE reaction.

C. Plasma cells secrete antibody but do not carry surface receptors that gliadin binds to trigger polyclonal secretion. The IgA in celiac is specifically directed at tissue transglutaminase, endomysium, and deamidated gliadin, made by gluten-specific B cells with T-cell help.

D. The pathway is tissue transglutaminase deamidating gliadin, the deamidated peptide binding HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 with high affinity, presentation to CD4+ T cells, a Th1 response, and then cytotoxic destruction of the villi alongside B-cell antibody production. The genetic restriction (DQ2 in about 95%, DQ8 in most of the rest) explains why celiac is genetically determined, since people without those alleles cannot form the high-affinity peptide-MHC complex.

E.  This describes innate activation by bacterial endotoxin through TLR4. The microbiome may modulate celiac, but the primary driver is gliadin presented through the adaptive pathway, and the HLA-DQ2 restriction with a gluten-specific T-cell response argues against an LPS mechanism.

References: Lebwohl, B., Sanders, D. S., & Green, P. H. R. (2018). Coeliac disease. The Lancet, 391(10115), 70–81. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31796-8Green, P. H. R., & Cellier, C. (2007). Celiac disease. New England Journal of Medicine, 357(17), 1731–1743.

Clinical vignette

Answer choices

A

Th2-mediated continuous mucosal inflammation limited to the colon starting at the rectum

⊘

B

⊘

C

Antibody-mediated complement-fixing destruction of colonic enterocytes

⊘

D

Mycobacterium tuberculosis colonization producing caseating granulomas

⊘

E

Answer: E

Concept explanation

In Crohn’s disease the inflammation burrows through the whole thickness of the wall, from the inner lining all the way out, which is what "transmural". Because the inflammation is full-thickness, it can tunnel out of the bowel and form fistulas (to skin, bladder, vagina, or another loop of gut), wall off into abscesses, and scar into strictures that obstruct. It can appear anywhere from mouth to anus, and it does so in patches, leaving normal "skip" areas between inflamed segments. The terminal ileum is the favorite site, and perianal disease (fistulas, fissures, the drainage in this patient) is a strong Crohn clue. The deep linear ulcers crisscrossing swollen mucosa give the "cobblestone" look on endoscopy, and the biopsy shows non-caseating granulomas.



The immune driving force is a Th1 and Th17 response, and a major susceptibility gene is NOD2 (CARD15), which normally helps cells sense bacterial cell-wall fragments inside the gut wall. When NOD2 works poorly, the handling of gut bacteria goes wrong, the mucosal barrier and innate response misfire, and a chronic granulomatous inflammation builds against the normal microbiota. This is why Crohn’s is best thought of as a disordered immune response to gut bacteria in a genetically susceptible host rather than an attack on a single self-antigen, and it explains the granulomas, which are organized collections of macrophages walling off a persistent stimulus.



NOD2 is best pictured as a smoke detector inside the cell that sniffs out a fragment of bacterial wall called muramyl dipeptide. When it works, it triggers a measured cleanup of the microbes that slip across the gut lining. When the gene is faulty that first response misfires, bacteria linger, and the immune system falls back on a cruder, smouldering reaction that walls the persistent stimulus off inside granulomas. So Crohn is less an attack on the body's own tissue than a botched negotiation with the normal gut bacteria.



Crohn’s also has a list of extraintestinal features: aphthous mouth ulcers, erythema nodosum (the tender shin nodules here), pyoderma gangrenosum, episcleritis and uveitis, a peripheral arthritis that correlates with bowel activity, and an axial arthritis or sacroiliitis that does not. Malabsorption is part of the picture too, with terminal ileal disease causing B12 deficiency and bile-acid malabsorption, and chronic inflammation raising the risk of gallstones and oxalate kidney stones. Smoking makes Crohn’s worse, which is the reverse of ulcerative colitis, where smoking is oddly protective.



Workup combines the endoscopic and histologic findings with raised inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) and a high fecal calprotectin that points to bowel inflammation rather than a functional disorder. Treatment steps up from corticosteroids for flares to steroid-sparing immunomodulators (azathioprine, methotrexate) and biologics (TNF inhibitors such as infliximab and adalimumab, the anti-integrin vedolizumab, and the anti-IL-12/23 ustekinumab), with surgery reserved for complications since, unlike ulcerative colitis, resection is not curative and disease tends to recur at the anastomosis.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Ulcerative colitis his a continuous mucosal (not transmural) inflammation that begins at the rectum and extends proximally within the colon only. This patient has skip lesions, terminal ileal disease, transmural granulomas, and a perianal fistula, which are Crohn features, not ulcerative colitis.

B. This describes an IgE and mast-cell, eosinophilic process such as eosinophilic gastroenteritis or an allergic reaction, not the Th1/Th17 granulomatous inflammation of Crohn.

C. Antibody and complement-mediated destruction of enterocytes, is not how Crohn works; the disease is driven by T cells and a disordered response to gut flora, not by a complement-fixing autoantibody against the lining.

D. Intestinal tuberculosis can mimic Crohn, including ileal involvement, but its granulomas are caseating and the organism is demonstrable. Crohn granulomas are non-caseating and no mycobacterium is found, which is the distinction the biopsy makes here.

E. Crohn disease is a Th1- and Th17-driven, transmural inflammation with non-caseating granulomas, and a major genetic association is with NOD2/CARD15 mutations that impair sensing of bacterial products in the gut wall. The skip lesions, cobblestoning, terminal ileal involvement, perianal fistula, and transmural granulomatous biopsy in this patient all fit.

Q29.  Ulcerative Colitis

Clinical vignette

A 26-year-old woman, a lifelong nonsmoker, presents with 3 months of bloody diarrhea with mucus, lower abdominal cramping, and a frequent urge to move her bowels even when little comes out. She has up to eight stools a day, including at night. Examination shows mild left-sided abdominal tenderness. Laboratory studies show a mild anemia and a raised CRP, with a positive atypical p-ANCA. The terminal ileum is normal. Biopsy shows mucosal and submucosal inflammation limited to the superficial layers, with crypt abscesses and crypt distortion, and no granulomas. Answer choices

A

Continuous mucosal inflammation extending proximally from the rectum and limited to the colon, with crypt abscesses

✓

⊘

⊘

⊘

E

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

If Crohn disease tunnels through the full thickness of the bowel wall in patches, ulcerative colitis does the opposite: it stays shallow, in the mucosa and submucosa, and it spreads as one continuous sheet. It begins at the rectum and marches proximally without skipping, and it never leaves the colon. How far it reaches defines the subtype, from proctitis (rectum only) to left-sided colitis to pancolitis (the whole colon). Because the inflammation is continuous and superficial, the symptoms are bloody diarrhea with mucus, lower abdominal cramping, and tenesmus, the constant urge to defecate that this patient describes.

On endoscopy the mucosa looks uniformly inflamed, friable, and granular, with loss of the normal vascular markings, and longstanding disease leaves pseudopolyps (islands of regenerating mucosa) and a smooth, featureless "lead-pipe" colon as the haustra disappear. The biopsy shows the inflammation confined to the superficial layers with crypt abscesses (neutrophils filling the crypts) and distorted, branched crypts, and there are no granulomas, which helps separate it from Crohn. An atypical p-ANCA is found in many patients and is a soft supporting clue. One memorable epidemiologic quirk is that smoking is protective in ulcerative colitis, the reverse of Crohn, and the disease sometimes flares when a patient quits.



The crypts are the test-tube-shaped glands that dip down into the colon lining and constantly renew it. In ulcerative colitis neutrophils pour into these glands and clog them, the crypt abscess, and the repeated injury and repair warp the once-orderly rows into branched, distorted shapes. Because the damage stays in this superficial mucosal layer and never tunnels through the wall, removing the colon can cure the disease, unlike full-thickness Crohn.



Ulcerative colitis carries its own set of extraintestinal features. Some track with bowel activity, like peripheral arthritis, erythema nodosum, and episcleritis, while others run their own course independent of the gut, including ankylosing spondylitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis. Primary sclerosing cholangitis deserves attention because of its tight link to ulcerative colitis and its added cancer risk. Two complications define the danger of the disease: toxic megacolon, a dilated, paralyzed colon at risk of perforation, and colorectal cancer, whose risk rises with the duration and extent of colitis, which is why patients undergo surveillance colonoscopy with biopsies after roughly 8 years of disease.



Treatment is built around 5-aminosalicylates (mesalamine) for mild disease and maintenance, corticosteroids for flares, and immunomodulators and biologics (TNF inhibitors, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, JAK inhibitors) for moderate-to-severe disease. What separates it from Crohn’s therapeutically is that colectomy is curative in ulcerative colitis, since the disease is limited to the colon.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Ulcerative colitis is a continuous mucosal and submucosal inflammation that starts at the rectum and extends proximally within the colon only, with crypt abscesses and distorted crypts and no granulomas. The rectal-onset continuous colitis, bloody diarrhea with tenesmus, atypical p-ANCA, and superficial inflammation in this patient all fit.

B. Crohn’s disease, with transmural inflammation, skip lesions, and non-caseating granulomas. This patient's inflammation is continuous, superficial, and limited to the colon with no granulomas, which is the opposite pattern.

C. Crohn’s disease, which can affect anywhere from mouth to anus and forms fistulas because the inflammation is full-thickness. Ulcerative colitis is confined to the colon and does not form fistulas.

D. Eosinophilic colitis is a IgE-associated process driven by dietary antigens, not the neutrophilic crypt-abscess colitis of ulcerative colitis.

E.  Intestinal tuberculosis, with caseating granulomas and a demonstrable mycobacterium. Ulcerative colitis has no granulomas and no infectious organism; its hallmark is crypt abscesses in continuous superficial colitis.

✓

Answer: B

Concept explanation

The immune system turns on the hepatocytes and drives a immune response leading to chronic hepatitis. Autoimmune hepatitis most often appears in women, can begin at almost any age, and ranges from an insidious fatigue-and-jaundice presentation to an acute hepatitis that can even tip into liver failure. The lab pattern is hepatocellular, with the transaminases (AST and ALT) far more elevated than the alkaline phosphatase, and a strikingly high polyclonal IgG.



There are two serologic types worth separating. Type 1, the common adult form, is marked by a positive ANA and anti-smooth muscle antibody (the antigen is actin), as in this patient. Type 2 is marked by anti-liver-kidney microsomal-1 (anti-LKM1) and anti-liver-cytosol-1 antibodies, tends to appear in children, and often runs a more aggressive course. Knowing which antibody belongs to which type, and separating both from the antibodies of the cholestatic liver diseases, is what usually gets tested.



The biopsy is characteristic. Interface hepatitis/piecemeal necorsis is a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate that crosses the limiting plate at the portal-parenchymal boundary and eats into the lobule, with plasma cells prominent and individual hepatocytes dropping out. This is a hepatocellular, interface pattern, which contrasts with the bile-duct-centered injury of primary biliary cholangitis and primary sclerosing cholangitis. Autoimmune hepatitis also occurs alongside other autoimmune diseases, including autoimmune thyroid disease, celiac disease, and type 1 diabetes, and it can overlap with the cholestatic autoimmune liver diseases.



Interface hepatitis names exactly where the fight happens. Liver cells are arranged in lobules, and a tidy boundary called the limiting plate separates each portal tract, which carries the bile duct and vessels, from the working liver tissue. In autoimmune hepatitis the lymphocytes and plasma cells spill across that boundary and chew into the liver cells just beyond it, nibbling the edge piece by piece, the piecemeal necrosis pathologists describe, and that location points to a hepatocyte-targeting disease rather than one aimed at the bile ducts.



Diagnosis is made by liver enzymes, high IgG, autoantibodies, exclusion of viral and toxic causes, and the interface hepatitis on biopsy, often using a scoring system. The disease responds well to immunosuppression: corticosteroids to induce remission, with azathioprine added as a steroid-sparing maintenance agent, and treatment is usually long-term. Untreated, it can progress to cirrhosis and its complications, so recognizing the type 1 pattern of ANA plus anti-smooth muscle antibody in a young woman with a hepatocellular hepatitis and high IgG is critical.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Primary sclerosing cholangitis contains an atypical p-ANCA and multifocal biliary strictures on imaging. This is a cholestatic, large-duct disease, not the hepatocellular interface hepatitis seen here.

B. Type 1 autoimmune hepatitis is defined by a positive ANA and anti-smooth muscle (anti-actin) antibody, which fits this young woman with a hepatocellular hepatitis (AST and ALT far above the alkaline phosphatase), a high polyclonal IgG, and interface hepatitis with plasma cells on biopsy.

C. Anti-tissue transglutaminase is the antibody of celiac disease and is unrelated to autoimmune hepatitis, although celiac can coexist with autoimmune liver disease. It does not explain a hepatocellular hepatitis with high IgG and interface hepatitis.

D. The anti-TSH receptor antibody belongs to Graves disease and has nothing to do with the liver. It would not produce hepatitis, high IgG, or the autoantibody pattern described.

E. Primary biliary cholangitis, where the anti-mitochondrial antibody attacks small intrahepatic bile ducts and produces a cholestatic pattern with a high alkaline phosphatase. This patient has a hepatocellular pattern with interface hepatitis, not a duct-centered cholestatic disease.

Clinical vignette

A 52-year-old woman presents with several months of fatigue and relentless itching that is worst at night and over her palms and soles. She has noticed yellowish plaques on her eyelids. She drinks no alcohol and takes no new medications, and she also reports dry eyes and a dry mouth. Examination shows xanthelasma and excoriations from scratching, with mild hepatomegaly and no jaundice. Laboratory studies: alkaline phosphatase markedly elevated, gamma-glutamyl transferase elevated, AST and ALT only mildly elevated, total bilirubin near the upper limit of normal, and a raised serum IgM. Answer choices

A

Anti-smooth muscle antibody

⊘

B

Atypical p-ANCA with a beaded biliary tree

⊘

C

Anti-centromere antibody

⊘

D

Anti-mitochondrial antibody

✓

E

Anti-liver-kidney microsomal-1 (anti-LKM1) antibody

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

Think of the bile duct system as the liver's plumbing, these tiny bile ducts drain bile out of the liver. In primary biliary cholangitis the immune system attacks the smallest of these, i.e. small intrahepatic bile ducts, and slowly destroys them. The classic patient is a middle-aged woman with fatigue and itching, and the pruritus is often the first and most miserable symptom, driven by retained bile components. As ducts are lost, bile backs up, so the lab pattern is cholestatic: a markedly raised alkaline phosphatase and GGT with only mild transaminase elevation, the reverse of the hepatocellular pattern in autoimmune hepatitis. Cholesterol cannot be excreted well, so it builds up and shows on the skin as xanthelasma around the eyes.



It helps to know where these enzymes come from, because the pattern is half the diagnosis. AST and ALT live inside hepatocytes, so when the liver cells themselves are injured those two spill into the blood and lead the picture, the hepatocellular pattern of autoimmune hepatitis. Alkaline phosphatase and GGT instead line the bile-duct cells, so when the ducts are the target, as here, those two climb far higher than the transaminases. Reading which pair is elevated tells you whether the attack is on the liver cells or on the plumbing.



The defining antibody is the anti-mitochondrial antibody, positive in around 95% of patients, alongside a raised IgM. The histology is a granulomatous destruction of small bile ducts (the florid duct lesion), a useful contrast with the large-duct strictures of primary sclerosing cholangitis and the hepatocyte-centered interface hepatitis of autoimmune hepatitis. Because bile is needed to absorb fat and fat-soluble vitamins, longstanding disease brings malabsorption of vitamins A, D, E, and K and a real risk of osteoporosis, so bone density and vitamin status are followed.



Primary biliary cholangitis occurs concomitantly with other autoimmune diseases, which is why this patient also has sicca symptoms. The common companions are Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto), and the limited cutaneous scleroderma (CREST) spectrum, and an overlap with CREST is common enough that anti-centromere antibodies can coexist, though they are not what defines the liver disease. The combination of itching, a cholestatic profile, and a positive anti-mitochondrial antibody in a middle-aged woman is the pattern to recognize.



Treatment is ursodeoxycholic acid as first-line, which improves the cholestasis and slows progression, with obeticholic acid or fibrates added for inadequate responders, and cholestyramine or other agents used to control the pruritus. End-stage disease is treated with liver transplantation, after which the disease can occasionally recur. The reason to identify it early is that ursodeoxycholic acid works best before cirrhosis develops, unlike its counterpart primary sclerosing cholangitis, where no medical therapy clearly changes the course.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Type 1 autoimmune hepatitis is a hepatocellular disease with transaminases far above the alkaline phosphatase and interface hepatitis on biopsy. This patient has a cholestatic, duct-centered picture, not interface hepatitis.

B. Primary sclerosing cholangitis contains an atypical p-ANCA and a beaded biliary tree on imaging from large-duct strictures. Primary biliary cholangitis attacks the small intrahepatic ducts and is defined by the anti-mitochondrial antibody, not p-ANCA.

C. Anti-centromere is seen in limited scleroderma (CREST), which overlaps with primary biliary cholangitis often enough that the two can coexist, but the antibody that defines this patient's liver disease is the anti-mitochondrial antibody.

D. The anti-mitochondrial antibody is positive in roughly 95% of primary biliary cholangitis and is the characteristic serology, fitting this middle-aged woman with fatigue, pruritus, xanthelasma, a cholestatic profile (high alkaline phosphatase and GGT with mild transaminases), and a raised IgM from autoimmune destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts.

E. Type 2 autoimmune hepatitis is marked by anti-LKM1 and usually seen in children, and it is a hepatocellular disease unlike this cholestatic one.

Q32.  Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis

Clinical vignette

A 41-year-old man with an 8-year history of ulcerative colitis presents with several months of fatigue, intermittent itching, and episodes of right upper quadrant discomfort. He has had no fever or biliary surgery. Examination shows mild scleral icterus. Laboratory studies reveal alkaline phosphatase markedly elevated, GGT elevated, AST and ALT mildly elevated, and total bilirubin mildly elevated. Anti-mitochondrial antibody is negative; an atypical p-ANCA is positive. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography shows multiple short strictures alternating with dilated segments throughout the intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts, giving a "beads-on-a-string" appearance. Which of the following findings is most characteristic of this patient's disease?

Answer choices

A

Anti-mitochondrial antibody with destruction of small intrahepatic bile ducts

⊘

B

Anti-smooth muscle antibody with interface hepatitis on biopsy

⊘

C

Anti-tissue transglutaminase antibody with villous atrophy

⊘

D

A single obstructing gallstone in the common bile duct causing reversible cholestasis

⊘

E

Multifocal intra- and extrahepatic biliary strictures ("beads-on-a-string") with onion-skin periductal fibrosis, strongly associated with ulcerative colitis

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

While primary biliary cholangitis goes after the smallest ducts, primary sclerosing cholangitis attacks the larger bile ducts, both inside and outside the liver, and scars them. The ducts develop a concentric "onion-skin" fibrosis around their walls, narrowing in some places and dilating in others, so an MRCP or ERCP shows the classic "beads-on-a-string" pattern of alternating strictures and dilations. The typical patient is a man, often in his thirties or forties, and the single most useful association is with inflammatory bowel disease, especially ulcerative colitis, which this patient has. Remember it both ways, patient with ulcerative colitis and a cholestatic liver profile should make you think of primary sclerosing cholangitis, and a new diagnosis of primary sclerosing cholangitis should prompt a look for inflammatory bowel disease.



The beaded look has a simple mechanical cause. Scarring clamps the duct down at intervals, and each tight ring acts like a dam: bile backs up just upstream and balloons that segment outward, then the next scar clamps again. Strung together, the alternating pinch and bulge gives the row-of-beads appearance on the cholangiogram, which is why imaging the ducts, rather than biopsy, is what clinches the diagnosis.



The lab pattern is cholestatic, like primary biliary cholangitis, with a high alkaline phosphatase and GGT and only mild transaminase elevation. Primary sclerosing cholangitis is anti-mitochondrial antibody negative and often atypical-p-ANCA positive, the reverse of the anti-mitochondrial-positive primary biliary cholangitis. The diagnosis rests mainly on the cholangiogram showing the beaded ducts rather than on biopsy, though biopsy can show the onion-skin fibrosis.



The reason primary sclerosing cholangitis is dangerous beyond the cholestasis is cancer. It carries a substantially increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma, and patients also face higher rates of gallbladder cancer and, in those with ulcerative colitis, colorectal cancer, so surveillance is part of management. Recurrent bacterial cholangitis and the slow progression to biliary cirrhosis add to the burden. A sudden worsening of jaundice or a dominant stricture raises concern for cholangiocarcinoma.



Unlike primary biliary cholangitis, where ursodeoxycholic acid is the initial treatment, no treatment has been shown to change the progression of primary sclerosing cholangitis; ursodeoxycholic acid is not proven to improve outcomes. Endoscopic dilation or stenting can relieve dominant strictures, and liver transplantation is the definitive option for end-stage disease, after which the disease can recur.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Primary biliary cholangitis attacks the small intrahepatic ducts and is defined by the anti-mitochondrial antibody. This patient's anti-mitochondrial antibody is negative and the disease involves large ducts, which is the opposite.

B. Autoimmune hepatitis is a hepatocellular disease with anti-smooth muscle antibody and interface hepatitis, not a large-duct cholestatic disease with beaded strictures.

C. This option describes celiac disease, with anti-tissue transglutaminase and villous atrophy. That is a small-bowel disease unrelated to the biliary strictures seen here.

D. Choledocholithiasis is a single obstructing common-duct stone causing reversible cholestasis, which would show one blockage rather than the diffuse, multifocal beaded strictures of primary sclerosing cholangitis.

E. Primary sclerosing cholangitis produces multifocal strictures and dilations of the intra- and extrahepatic ducts ("beads-on-a-string") with onion-skin periductal fibrosis, and it is strongly associated with ulcerative colitis. This patient's ulcerative colitis, cholestatic labs, negative anti-mitochondrial antibody, positive atypical p-ANCA, and beaded cholangiogram fit.

Q33.  Pernicious Anemia (Autoimmune Gastritis)

Clinical vignette

A 62-year-old woman presents with several months of fatigue, a sore, smooth, red tongue, and numbness and tingling in both feet that has progressed to an unsteady gait. She has trouble sensing where her feet are in the dark. Examination shows pallor, a beefy red tongue, reduced vibration and position sense in the lower limbs, and a positive Romberg sign. Laboratory studies: hemoglobin 9.0 g/dL with MCV 112 fL (macrocytic), a peripheral smear showing hypersegmented neutrophils and oval macrocytes, low serum vitamin B12, and elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies are positive, the serum gastrin is high, and endoscopic biopsy shows atrophic gastritis of the gastric body with loss of parietal cells. Which of the following best explains this patient's findings?

Answer choices

A

Autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells with loss of intrinsic factor, causing vitamin B12 malabsorption

✓

B

Folate deficiency from poor dietary intake

⊘

C

Iron deficiency from chronic gastrointestinal blood loss

⊘

D

Gluten-triggered villous atrophy impairing nutrient absorption

⊘

E

Hereditary spherocytosis with extravascular hemolysis

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

The stomach lining contains different specialized cells, the parietal cells in the body and fundus do two jobs; they make stomach acid and they make intrinsic factor, a protein that vitamin B12 must bind in order to be absorbed in the terminal ileum. In autoimmune gastritis the immune system attacks these parietal cells, and as they are destroyed both jobs fail. Acid production drops (achlorhydria) and intrinsic factor is no longer produced, hence dietary B12 can no longer be absorbed. The resulting B12 deficiency is what is called pernicious anemia, and because the body stores years of B12 in the liver, the deficiency develops slowly over a long time.



Two antibodies show up. Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies are the more specific of the two and block B12 from binding intrinsic factor, while anti-parietal cell antibodies are more sensitive but less specific. The destruction of parietal cells also removes acid's normal brake on gastrin, so gastrin levels climb (hypergastrinemia), and the chronic stimulation of enterochromaffin-like cells raises the risk of gastric carcinoid tumors, while the atrophic gastritis itself raises the risk of gastric adenocarcinoma over time.



The clinical picture has two arms, and the neurologic arm is the one that distinguishes B12 deficiency from other megaloblastic anemias and the hematologic arm, which is a macrocytic, megaloblastic anemia: a high MCV, oval macrocytes, and hypersegmented neutrophils on the smear, with a sore, smooth, beefy-red tongue (glossitis). The neurologic arm is subacute combined degeneration, where B12's role in myelin maintenance fails and the dorsal columns (vibration and position sense, with a positive Romberg) and the lateral corticospinal tracts degenerate, giving the numbness, unsteady gait, and proprioceptive loss seen here. Biochemically, B12 is low and both methylmalonic acid and homocysteine are elevated, which is the pattern that separates B12 deficiency from folate deficiency, where the methylmalonic acid stays normal.



The split between B12 and folate comes down to two reactions. B12 is a cofactor for two enzymes: methionine synthase, which it shares with folate to recycle homocysteine, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, which folate has nothing to do with. Lose B12 and both reactions back up, so homocysteine and methylmalonic acid both rise. Lose folate and only the shared reaction stalls, so homocysteine rises but methylmalonic acid stays normal. That lone methylmalonic acid is the tiebreaker, and the stranded methylmalonyl pathway is also thought to derail myelin, which is why only B12 deficiency carries the neurologic damage.



Pernicious anemia is organ-specific autoimmunity and occurs with the similar organ-specific diseases, including Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes, vitiligo, and Addison disease. Treatment is vitamin B12 replacement, given parenterally (intramuscular) because the gut can no longer absorb it through the usual intrinsic-factor route, with high-dose oral B12 an option in some cases. Replacing B12 reverses the anemia reliably, but longstanding neurologic damage may only partly recover, which is why early recognition matters, and patients require ongoing monitoring for the gastric cancer risk.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells removes intrinsic factor (and acid), so vitamin B12 cannot be absorbed, producing a megaloblastic anemia (high MCV, hypersegmented neutrophils) and subacute combined degeneration (dorsal column and corticospinal signs). The positive anti-intrinsic factor antibody, high gastrin, atrophic gastritis with parietal-cell loss, low B12, and elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine all fit.

B. Folate deficiency also causes a megaloblastic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, but it does not cause the neurologic findings of subacute combined degeneration, and the methylmalonic acid would be normal rather than elevated. The neurologic signs and high methylmalonic acid point to B12, not folate.

C. Iron deficiency causes a microcytic anemia with a low MCV, the opposite of this patient's macrocytosis, and it does not cause the neurologic findings or the autoantibodies described.

D. Celiac disease can impair absorption and cause deficiencies, but the specific combination here, anti-intrinsic factor antibodies, atrophic gastritis of the gastric body with parietal-cell loss, and high gastrin, points to autoimmune gastritis rather than small-bowel villous atrophy.

E. Hereditary spherocytosis causes a hemolytic anemia with spherocytes and splenomegaly, not a macrocytic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils, low B12, and neurologic signs. The mechanism and the smear are different.

Q34.  Multiple Sclerosis

Clinical vignette

A 28-year-old woman is evaluated for a neurologic episode. This is the 4th one in the last 18 months. The first was painful unilateral vision loss with central scotoma (right optic neuritis, resolved over weeks). The second was bilateral lower extremity weakness with transient bladder urgency (partially resolved). The third was right-hand numbness lasting 3 weeks (fully resolved). She now presents with 6 days of vertigo, oscillopsia, and gait ataxia. Examination: bilateral internuclear ophthalmoplegia, mild bilateral leg spasticity with brisk reflexes, dysmetria on heel-to-shin testing. MRI of brain and cervical spinal cord shows multiple ovoid periventricular and juxtacortical white matter lesions; one cervical cord lesion enhances after gadolinium administration while three brain lesions are non-enhancing. Lumbar puncture shows 4 oligoclonal IgG bands present in CSF but not in serum, elevated CSF IgG index of 0.92 (normal <0.7), mildly elevated CSF protein, normal cell count. Which of the following best explains the pathogenesis of her neurologic disease?

Answer choices

A

Cytomegalovirus reactivation produces direct cytopathic damage to CNS neurons

⊘

B

Autoreactive CD4+ Th1/Th17 cells cross the blood-brain barrier and recruit macrophages that strip myelin from CNS axons

✓

C

Mitochondrial dysfunction in retinal ganglion cells (Leber hereditary optic neuropathy)

⊘

D

Embolic occlusion of cerebral small vessels

⊘

E

Anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) IgG fixes complement on oligodendrocytes (different disease)

~

Answer: B

Concept explanation

Picture the nerve fibers in the brain and spinal cord as electrical wires, each wrapped in insulating tape called myelin. The tape lets the signal jump from gap to gap (the nodes of Ranvier) so messages travel fast and accurately. In multiple sclerosis the immune system mistakes that insulation for a threat and strips it off in patches throughout the central nervous system, leaving bare wires that misfire or stop conducting. Which symptom appears depends on which wire was stripped: optic nerve gives vision loss, cervical cord gives limb weakness, brainstem gives vertigo and double vision, and cerebellum gives ataxia. Over years the bare patches add up and the deficits accumulate. The classic patient is a young woman, and this is the most common demographic on a clinical vignette.



The disease is defined by two kinds of spread. Dissemination in space means lesions in different parts of the central nervous system, and dissemination in time means lesions of different ages, some new and enhancing, some old and quiet. The McDonald criteria use clinical attacks plus MRI to show both. Three findings carry the diagnosis. MRI shows ovoid lesions in periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal-cord locations, with gadolinium enhancement marking the active ones. The CSF shows oligoclonal bands present in the CSF but not the serum (intrathecal antibody production) along with a raised IgG index. Visually evoked potentials can show a prolonged P100 latency from a past optic neuritis even when the eye is currently fine.



Oligoclonal bands are worth decoding, since they anchor the diagnosis. When the lab runs CSF and blood side by side on a gel, antibodies of a single clone show up as discrete bands. Finding several bands in the spinal fluid that are absent from the blood means a small set of B-cell clones is making antibody inside the central nervous system itself, behind the blood-brain barrier, rather than antibody leaking in from the circulation. That trapped, locally produced signal is what the test is built to catch.



The main driver is T-cell-mediated. Autoreactive CD4+ T cells specific for myelin antigens (myelin basic protein, proteolipid protein, MOG) slip across a leaky blood-brain barrier, with Th1 cells releasing IFN-gamma and Th17 cells releasing IL-17, and they recruit macrophages that physically peel myelin off the axons. CD8+ T cells, B cells, and antibody add to the damage. Genetic risk centers on HLA-DR2 (DRB1*15:01). Environmental risks include vitamin D deficiency (the disease is more common at higher latitudes), prior Epstein-Barr virus infection (likely a necessary trigger), smoking, and adolescent obesity.



The course comes in flavors: relapsing-remitting (discrete attacks with recovery, as in this patient), secondary progressive (gradual decline after an initial relapsing phase), and primary progressive (gradual decline from the start, in a minority). Acute attacks are shortened with high-dose IV methylprednisolone, which speeds recovery without changing the long-term course. Disease-modifying therapy ranges from older injectables (interferon-beta, glatiramer) through oral agents (S1P modulators like fingolimod, dimethyl fumarate) to high-efficacy antibodies (natalizumab against alpha-4 integrin, ocrelizumab against CD20, alemtuzumab against CD52).

Why each option is right or wrong

A. CMV can cause CNS infection, but it presents as an encephalitis with altered mental status and seizures, usually in immunocompromised patients, not the relapsing multifocal demyelination with oligoclonal bands seen here.

B. The pathogenesis is autoreactive CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells crossing the blood-brain barrier and recruiting macrophages that strip myelin from central nervous system axons. The dissemination in time and space (optic nerve, brainstem, cord, cerebellum), the periventricular and juxtacortical lesions with mixed enhancement, and the CSF oligoclonal bands with a high IgG index confirm relapsing-remitting MS in the classic young-woman presentation.

C. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy is a mitochondrial disorder causing painless bilateral subacute vision loss, mostly in young men. It affects only the optic nerves and would not explain cord, brainstem, and cerebellar involvement.

D.  An ischemic stroke produces acute deficits in arterial territories rather than ovoid periventricular demyelinating lesions. Multiple strokes in a young woman would suggest a clotting disorder or vasculitis, not relapsing demyelination.

E. This option describes neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder or anti-MOG disease, which are related but distinct. Neuromyelitis optica targets aquaporin-4 and causes severe, often bilateral optic neuritis with long cord lesions spanning three or more segments, usually without oligoclonal bands. This patient's relapsing pattern, shorter cord lesion, and positive CSF bands point to MS, which is why this is a near-miss rather than the answer.

Q35.  Myasthenia Gravis

Clinical vignette

A 38-year-old woman presents with 6 months of progressively worsening bilateral eyelid drooping that is mild on awakening, worsens through the day, and is severe by evening. She has double vision when reading for more than 30 minutes, difficulty chewing tough foods, and a nasal-quality voice toward the end of long conversations. She reports that symptoms improve markedly after a 1-hour nap. Examination on a sustained upward gaze for 60 seconds: progressive ptosis develops on both sides (fatigable ptosis). She has weakness of deltoid and triceps that worsens with repetitive lifting and improves after brief rest. Pupillary responses, deep tendon reflexes, and sensation are normal. Anti-acetylcholine receptor (anti-AChR) antibodies positive at 8.4 nmol/L (markedly elevated; normal <0.4). Repetitive nerve stimulation shows a 35% decrement of compound muscle action potential at low frequency (3 Hz). Chest CT shows a 4-cm anterior mediastinal mass. Which of the following best explains the mechanism of her weakness?

Answer choices

A

Antibodies against voltage-gated calcium channels at presynaptic motor terminals block acetylcholine release

⊘

B

Mitochondrial mutations impair ATP production in muscle fibers

⊘

C

IgG antibodies bind the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor on muscle, blocking acetylcholine binding and triggering internalization of the receptor

✓

D

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells directly destroy muscle fibers

⊘

E

Antibodies against myelin-associated glycoprotein damage peripheral nerve myelin

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Think of the connection between nerve and muscle as a relay station. The nerve ending releases acetylcholine, which floats across a tiny gap (synapse) and lands on receptors on the muscle side to trigger a contraction. In myasthenia gravis the body makes IgG antibodies against the muscle's nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. The antibodies block acetylcholine from binding, cross-link and internalize the receptors so there are fewer of them, and fix complement that damages the junction. Each nerve signal now lands on a depleted receptor field, and as repeated use drains the readily available acetylcholine, the muscle fails progressively. That is why the hallmark is fatigability: strength is decent after rest and fades with use, so the ptosis and double vision are mild in the morning and worse by evening, and improve again after a nap.



There is a built-in margin at the junction worth understanding. Normally each nerve impulse releases far more acetylcholine than the bare minimum needed to fire the muscle, a cushion called the safety factor. With receptors blocked and thinned out, that cushion is gone, so the first few contractions still work but the normal run-down of acetylcholine with repeated firing now drops the signal below threshold and the muscle quits. That same fade is what the nerve test captures: stimulate the nerve repeatedly and the muscle response shrinks step by step, the decremental pattern.



The weakness seen in myasthenia gravis favors specific muscle groups. Ocular and bulbar muscles are hit early, giving fatigable ptosis, diplopia, trouble chewing, and a nasal voice toward the end of a conversation, and proximal limb muscles tire with repetition. The pupils are spared, sensation is normal, and reflexes are preserved, which separates it from other causes of weakness. The most dangeours manifestation of this disease is myasthenic crisis, when respiratory muscle weakness threatens breathing and the patient needs ventilatory support.



Many patients with myasthenia gravis also have thymic hyperplasia, and around 10 to 15% have a thymoma, the anterior mediastinal mass seen here, so everyone with myasthenia gets chest imaging to look for one. Most patients are anti-AChR positive; a subset instead carry anti-MuSK antibodies (against muscle-specific kinase) and tend to have more bulbar and respiratory weakness with less response to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors. On testing, repetitive nerve stimulation at low frequency shows a decremental response as the junction fatigues, and single-fiber EMG is the most sensitive test. In contrast, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is where the antibody is against presynaptic calcium channels, strength improves with use, and the stimulation pattern is incremental.



Treatment works on several levels. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) keep acetylcholine around longer and give symptomatic relief. Immunosuppression (corticosteroids, azathioprine, mycophenolate, and rituximab in refractory disease) addresses the underlying autoimmunity. 



Thymectomy helps, clearly so when a thymoma is present and also in many anti-AChR patients without one. A crisis is treated with IVIG or plasma exchange plus airway support. As a precautionary measure, certain drugs, including aminoglycosides and beta-blockers, can worsen myasthenic weakness and are avoided.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. In Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome, the antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels reduce acetylcholine release. There, strength improves with repeated use and stimulation gives an incremental response, the opposite of this patient's fatigable, decremental pattern.

B. In mitochondrial myopathy, an inherited energy-production defect, not an antibody disease, and it would not produce a positive anti-AChR antibody, a decremental stimulation response, or a thymoma.

C. In myasthenia gravis, IgG antibodies bind the postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, blocking acetylcholine, internalizing the receptor, and fixing complement at the junction. The fatigable ptosis and bulbar weakness that worsen through the day and improve with rest, the positive anti-AChR antibody, the decremental response on repetitive stimulation, and the thymoma all fit.

D. T-cell-mediated destruction of muscle fibers is seen in an inflammatory myopathy, which causes fixed proximal weakness with a raised CK, not the fatigable junctional weakness with antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor seen here.

E. This describes an antibody-mediated peripheral demyelinating neuropathy, which causes sensory loss and areflexia rather than fatigable weakness of ocular and bulbar muscles with normal sensation and reflexes.

References: Gilhus, N. E. (2016). Myasthenia gravis. New England Journal of Medicine, 375(26), 2570–2581.

Q36.  Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome

Clinical vignette

A 64-year-old man with a 40-pack-year smoking history presents with 3 months of difficulty rising from a chair and climbing stairs. He notes a dry mouth, constipation, and erectile dysfunction. He says that when he first grips an object his hand feels weak, but the grip strengthens over a few seconds of effort. Examination shows proximal lower-extremity weakness and reduced deep tendon reflexes that become detectable again immediately after 15 seconds of sustained muscle contraction. Ocular and bulbar muscles are relatively spared. Antibodies against presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels are positive, and repetitive nerve stimulation shows a low baseline response with a marked incremental increase at high frequency. A chest CT reveals a hilar mass. Which of the following best explains the mechanism of this patient's weakness?

Answer choices

A

IgG against the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor blocks and internalizes the receptor

⊘

B

Antibodies against muscle-specific kinase (MuSK) at the postsynaptic membrane

⊘

C

Cytotoxic T cells destroying muscle fibers

⊘

D

Antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels reduce acetylcholine release, with strength improving on repeated use

✓

E

Anti-ganglioside antibodies demyelinating peripheral nerves after an infection

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

If myasthenia gravis is a problem on the muscle side of the neuromuscular junction, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is a problem on the nerve side. To release acetylcholine, the nerve terminal has to let calcium in through voltage-gated calcium channels. In this syndrome the body makes antibodies against those presynaptic P/Q-type calcium channels, so less calcium enters and less acetylcholine is released with each impulse. The result is proximal weakness, mostly in the legs, with trouble rising from a chair or climbing stairs.



The clue that separates it from myasthenia gravis is how the weakness behaves with use. Here, repeated activity builds up calcium in the terminal and more acetylcholine gets released, so strength improves with effort, and depressed reflexes can reappear right after a sustained contraction (post-exercise facilitation). On nerve testing this shows up as a low baseline response that climbs with high-frequency stimulation, an incremental pattern, which is the mirror image of the decremental response in myasthenia gravis. Ocular and bulbar muscles, so prominent in myasthenia, are relatively spared here.



A second distinguishing feature is autonomic involvement, since those same calcium channels sit on autonomic nerve endings. Patients get a dry mouth, constipation, and erectile dysfunction, none of which are part of myasthenia gravis. Putting it all together, the presentation usually looks like proximal leg weakness that improves with use, depressed reflexes, autonomic symptoms, and an incremental stimulation response.



More than half of cases are paraneoplastic, and the usual culprit is small cell lung cancer, which is why a smoker with this presentation needs a careful evaluation for cancer (the hilar mass here). The calcium-channel antibody is often present before the tumor is found. Treatment starts with finding and treating the underlying cancer, which can improve the weakness on its own. Symptomatic therapy uses amifampridine (3,4-diaminopyridine), which prolongs the nerve action potential and boosts acetylcholine release, and immunotherapy (IVIG, plasma exchange, immunosuppressants) is added if needed.



The lung-cancer link is not a coincidence but the actual trigger. Small cell lung cancer arises from neuroendocrine cells, and those tumor cells happen to stud their surface with the very same P/Q-type calcium channels found on nerve terminals. The immune system, attacking the tumor, raises antibodies against those channels, and they cross-react with the identical channels at the neuromuscular junction. That is why the antibody often shows up before the cancer is found, and why hunting for the tumor is the first move.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Myasthenia gravis is a postsynaptic acetylcholine-receptor disease where strength fades with use and stimulation is decremental, the opposite of this patient's facilitation and incremental response, and without prominent autonomic features.

B. Anti-MuSK subtype of myasthenia gravis is still a postsynaptic disease with fatigable, often bulbar, weakness, not the presynaptic calcium-channel problem with facilitation seen here.

C. During inflammatory myopathy, T cells destroy muscle and cause fixed proximal weakness with a high CK, without the autonomic symptoms, antibody, or incremental stimulation pattern of this syndrome.

D. Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is caused by antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, which cut acetylcholine release. Repeated use builds up calcium and improves strength, reflexes facilitate after exercise, autonomic symptoms appear, and high-frequency stimulation gives an incremental response, all in a smoker with a lung mass, which fits the paraneoplastic small cell lung cancer association.

E. Guillain-Barré syndrome is a post-infectious demyelinating neuropathy with ascending weakness and areflexia, not a proximal weakness that improves with use and is tied to a lung cancer.

Q37.  Guillain-Barré Syndrome

Clinical vignette

A 32-year-old man presents with 4 days of progressive weakness that began in his feet and has climbed to involve both legs and now his hands. Three weeks earlier he had several days of bloody diarrhea that resolved on its own. Examination shows symmetric weakness that is worse distally, with absent deep tendon reflexes throughout and only mild sensory complaints. Over the next day his breathing becomes shallow and his forced vital capacity is falling, and his blood pressure and heart rate swing between extremes. Lumbar puncture shows a CSF protein of 110 mg/dL with only 2 white cells per microliter. Nerve conduction studies show a demyelinating pattern with slowed conduction and conduction block. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

Answer choices

A

High-dose intravenous corticosteroids

⊘

B

An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor such as pyridostigmine

⊘

C

Broad-spectrum antibiotics for the resolved diarrheal illness

⊘

D

Riluzole

⊘

E

Intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, with close monitoring of respiratory function

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Picture the peripheral nerves as the same insulated wires from the multiple sclerosis question, except now the attack is outside the brain and spinal cord, on the nerve roots and peripheral nerves. Guillain-Barré syndrome is an acute autoimmune attack on peripheral myelin, and it usually follows an infection by one to three weeks. The classic trigger is Campylobacter jejuni, the cause of this patient's recent bloody diarrhea, and others include CMV, EBV, Mycoplasma, and some respiratory viruses. The mechanism is molecular mimicry: antibodies raised against the microbe cross-react with gangliosides on peripheral nerves, so the immune system turns on the nerves once the infection is gone.



The signature is an ascending, symmetric weakness that starts in the legs and moves upward, with areflexia (lost reflexes) and only mild sensory symptoms despite prominent weakness. Two complications can arise out of this syndrome. The first is respiratory failure; weakness can climb to the diaphragm, so forced vital capacity and inspiratory force are tracked, and a falling FVC is the trigger to move the patient to intensive care and intubate before a crash. The second is autonomic instability, with swings in blood pressure, heart rate, and arrhythmias, which can be life-threatening on their own.



Two test findings aid in the diagnosis. The CSF shows albuminocytologic dissociation, a high protein with a near-normal cell count, because the inflamed nerve roots leak protein without much cellular response. Nerve conduction studies show a demyelinating pattern with slowed conduction and conduction block. GBS also has another variant known as Miller Fisher syndrome, which is a triad of ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia, tied to the anti-GQ1b antibody.



The Latin name just describes a mismatch. In most infections of the nervous system the spinal fluid fills with cells and protein together. Here the nerve roots are inflamed and leaky enough to spill protein into the fluid, but the process is autoimmune rather than infectious, so very few white cells follow. High protein with an almost normal cell count, albumin up but cells not, is the dissociation, and it is one of the classic clues that the problem is Guillain-Barré rather than an infection.



Guillain-Barré is treated with IVIG or plasma exchange, and the two are about equally effective, while corticosteroids do not help and are not used, which is a distractor because steroids work in so many other autoimmune diseases. As important as the immunotherapy is the supportive care: monitoring and protecting the airway, managing autonomic swings, and preventing the complications of immobility. Most patients recover over weeks to months, though some are left with residual weakness.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Corticosteroids do not improve outcomes in Guillain-Barré syndrome and are not part of treatment, even though they help many other autoimmune conditions.

B. An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor does nothing for a demyelinating neuropathy with areflexia and would not address the immune attack on peripheral myelin.

C. The diarrheal illness has already resolved, and the current problem is the post-infectious immune attack on nerves, not an ongoing infection, so antibiotics do not treat it.

D. Riluzole is used in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a progressive motor neuron disease with upper and lower motor neuron signs and no sensory involvement or albuminocytologic dissociation, which is a different disease from this acute post-infectious neuropathy.

E. Guillain-Barré syndrome is treated with IVIG or plasma exchange, with close monitoring of respiratory function because ascending weakness can reach the diaphragm. The post-Campylobacter onset, ascending symmetric weakness with areflexia, albuminocytologic dissociation, and a demyelinating nerve conduction pattern all fit, and the falling vital capacity makes airway vigilance the priority.

References: Willison, H. J., Jacobs, B. C., & van Doorn, P. A. (2016). Guillain-Barré syndrome. The Lancet, 388(10045), 717–727.

Q38.  Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder

Clinical vignette

A 35-year-old woman presents with 1 week of severe vision loss in both eyes at the same time, along with progressive weakness and numbness of both legs and urinary retention. A month earlier she had several days of intractable hiccups and vomiting that no one could explain. MRI of the spine shows a continuous cord lesion extending over five vertebral segments, and MRI of the brain is relatively unremarkable. Lumbar puncture shows a pleocytosis but no oligoclonal bands. Serum testing is positive for anti-aquaporin-4 IgG. Which of the following best characterizes the antibody driving this patient's disease?

Answer choices

A

Anti-aquaporin-4 IgG directed against the astrocyte water channel, causing complement-mediated injury

✓

B

Autoreactive Th1/Th17 cells stripping myelin, with CSF oligoclonal bands

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Neuromyelitis optica closely resembles multiple sclerosis (MS), but it is a different disease with a different target. Instead of attacking myelin, the antibody here goes after aquaporin-4, a water channel that sits on astrocytes, the support cells of the central nervous system. Aquaporin-4 is densely expressed in the optic nerves, the spinal cord, and a region of the brainstem called the area postrema, which is why the disease strikes those places. The antibody is IgG1, so it fixes complement and damages astrocytes, with myelin loss being secondary.



This is worth contrasting with multiple sclerosis at the cellular level. Aquaporin-4 is a water channel packed onto astrocytes, the support cells that anchor the blood-brain barrier and tend the neurons, with the densest coverage at the foot processes wrapping small vessels. The antibody lands there and, being a complement-fixing subclass, summons the complement cascade to punch holes in the astrocyte. So neuromyelitis optica injures the support cell first and strips myelin only as collateral damage, the reverse of multiple sclerosis, where myelin is the primary target.





The clinical picture is more severe and more focused than multiple sclerosis. Optic neuritis is often bilateral and simultaneous and tends to leave worse vision than an episode of multiple sclerosis. The myelitis is a longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis, meaning a single cord lesion that runs over three or more vertebral segments, which is unusual in MS where cord lesions are short. A third, almost specific, presentation is the area postrema syndrome: bouts of intractable hiccups, nausea, and vomiting, which is also the unexplained episode this patient had a month earlier.



Treatments differ and some MS drugs make neuromyelitis optica worse. The pointers toward neuromyelitis optica are the anti-aquaporin-4 antibody, the long cord lesion, severe and often bilateral optic neuritis, an area postrema syndrome, a relatively normal brain MRI early on, and the usual absence of CSF oligoclonal bands (which are typically present in MS). A related antibody, anti-MOG, defines a separate overlapping disorder with its own course.



Treatment splits into acute and maintenance. An acute attack gets high-dose IV steroids, with plasma exchange added when the response is poor. Long-term, the goal is to prevent relapses, which can be disabling, using B-cell depletion with rituximab or one of the newer targeted agents: eculizumab (against complement C5), satralizumab (against the IL-6 receptor), or inebilizumab (against CD19).

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Neuromyelitis optica is driven by anti-aquaporin-4 IgG against the astrocyte water channel, with complement-mediated astrocyte injury. The simultaneous bilateral optic neuritis, the longitudinally extensive cord lesion over five segments, the preceding area postrema syndrome (intractable hiccups and vomiting), the absence of oligoclonal bands, and the positive antibody all fit.

B. Multiple sclerosis is T-cell-mediated with short cord lesions and CSF oligoclonal bands, whereas this patient has a long cord lesion, no bands, and a positive aquaporin-4 antibody, which is why MS is the near-miss rather than the answer.

C. Myasthenia gravis is a neuromuscular junction disorder causing fatigable weakness, not optic neuritis and transverse myelitis

D. Guillain-Barré syndrome is a post-infectious demyelinating neuropathy with ascending weakness and areflexia, which does not cause optic neuritis or a long central cord lesion.

E. This option describes a paraneoplastic encephalomyelitis with anti-Hu antibodies from small cell lung cancer, which is a different antibody and clinical setting and does not produce the aquaporin-4-driven optic-spinal pattern seen here.

A 24-year-old woman is brought in by her family. The husband noted that since that last 3 weeks, there was a personality change in her that began with anxiety and paranoia and progressed to disorganized behavior and hallucinations, prompting an initial psychiatric admission. A

Herpes simplex virus replication in the temporal lobes

B

An ovarian teratoma, which should be identified and removed, with antibodies against the NMDA receptor

✓

⊘

D

⊘

E

A small cell lung cancer producing anti-Hu antibodies

Answer: B

Concept explanation

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis usually involves previously well young person, often a young woman, develops psychiatric symptoms and then neurologic ones over a few weeks. The antibody targets the NR1 subunit of the NMDA receptor, a glutamate receptor that is central to memory, behavior, and movement. 



The symptom parade maps onto what the receptor normally does. The NMDA receptor is the brain's main excitatory switch for glutamate and is essential for laying down memory and tuning the circuits that govern mood, behavior, and movement. When antibodies pull these receptors off the neuron surface, those circuits lose their balance: the frontal and limbic systems misfire into psychosis and seizures, the movement circuits throw out orofacial dyskinesias, and the brainstem autonomic centers swing the heart rate and blood pressure. One receptor removed from many regions is why the illness looks like several diseases at once.



The course usually starts with a viral-like prodrome, then a psychiatric phase that looks like new schizophrenia or a mood disorder, with anxiety, paranoia, delusions, and hallucinations, which is why so many patients first seek consultation in psychiatry. Within days to weeks the neurologic features develop such as, seizures, a movement disorder with orofacial dyskinesias (the lip-smacking and choreoathetoid movements here), a decreasing level of consciousness, and autonomic instability with swings in heart rate and blood pressure and sometimes central hypoventilation. That progression from psychiatric to seizures to dyskinesias to autonomic dysfunction in a young woman is a classical pattern of the disease.



The association that the question is testing is with an ovarian teratoma. A large share of young women with this encephalitis harbor a teratoma whose tissue expresses NMDA receptors and is thought to set off the antibody response, so finding and removing the tumor is part of treatment. Investigations therefore include pelvic imaging for a teratoma, CSF showing a lymphocytic pleocytosis, detection of the anti-NMDA receptor antibody in CSF and serum, and an EEG that can show a pattern called extreme delta brush. One nuance worth knowing is that herpes simplex encephalitis can trigger a secondary anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis weeks later, so a relapse of symptoms after treated HSV should raise the possibility.



Primary management is tumor removal if present, plus immunotherapy. First-line drugs are corticosteroids, IVIG, and plasma exchange, with rituximab or cyclophosphamide as second-line for patients who do not respond. With tumor removal and immunotherapy, many patients recover substantially, though it can take months and relapses occur, so the takeaway is to recognize the syndrome early, look hard for the teratoma, and start immunotherapy rather than treating it as a primary psychiatric illness.

A. Herpes simplex encephalitis can trigger a secondary anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis weeks after the infection, but the underlying tumor association being sought here is the ovarian teratoma, and this patient's subacute psychiatric-to-dyskinetic course with the antibody points to the autoimmune encephalitis itself.

B. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is strongly associated with an ovarian teratoma in young women, and the tumor should be sought with pelvic imaging and removed as part of treatment. The progression from psychiatric symptoms to seizures, orofacial dyskinesias, and autonomic instability, with anti-NMDA receptor antibodies in CSF and serum, is the classic picture.

C. Neuromyelitis optica, an aquaporin-4 disease of the optic nerves and spinal cord, not a psychiatric-and-seizure encephalitis with orofacial dyskinesias.

D. Prion disease such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease can cause a rapidly progressive dementia with myoclonus, usually in older patients, and is untreatable, unlike this immunotherapy-responsive encephalitis.

Q40.  Pemphigus Vulgaris

Clinical vignette

A 48-year-old man presents with 4 months of progressive painful erosions in the mouth that have prevented him from eating solid food. Over the last 6 weeks, he has developed multiple flaccid bullae on the chest, back, and scalp. The bullae rupture easily, leaving areas that are slow to heal. Examination shows extensive oral mucosal erosions and multiple flaccid bullae on a non-inflamed base. Biopsy of a fresh blister shows intraepidermal acantholysis just above the basal cell layer, producing a suprabasal cleft with the basal cells still attached to the basement membrane (the classic 'row of tombstones' appearance). Direct immunofluorescence shows IgG and C3 deposited in an intercellular ('fishnet' or 'chicken-wire') pattern throughout the epidermis. Which of the following is the molecular target of the pathogenic antibodies?

Answer choices

A

BP180 (BPAG2) and BP230 — hemidesmosomal proteins anchoring epidermis to dermis

⊘

B

Epidermal transglutaminase (TG3) at the dermal papillae

⊘

C

Desmoglein-3 (and sometimes desmoglein-1) — desmosomal cadherins providing intercellular adhesion between keratinocytes

✓

D

Laminin-332 of the basement membrane zone

⊘

E

Type VII collagen — anchoring fibrils of the basement membrane

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Picture the epidermis as a brick wall, where each brick is a keratinocyte and the bricks are held together by desmosomes. The “rivet” proteins are cadherins called desmogleins. Desmoglein-3 holds cells together in the lower epidermis and in mucous membranes like the mouth, while desmoglein-1 does the same job in the upper layers of the skin. In pemphigus vulgaris, autoantibodies dissolve the desmoglein-3 rivets, and sometimes desmoglein-1 too, so the cells lose their grip on each other and pull apart, a process called acantholysis. The split forms within the epidermis itself, producing flaccid bullae that are too fragile to stay intact and quickly rupture into painful raw erosions.



There is a neat rule that predicts where the blisters land, called desmoglein compensation. Skin carries both rivets, desmoglein-1 up high and desmoglein-3 down low, so if antibodies hit only desmoglein-3 the upper desmoglein-1 can still hold the skin together and disease stays in the mouth, where desmoglein-1 is scarce. Mucosa relies almost entirely on desmoglein-3, so it blisters first and alone. Once anti-desmoglein-1 antibodies appear as well, the skin loses its backup and flaccid blisters spread, which is exactly the mucosa-then-skin march this patient shows.



Because desmoglein-3 is concentrated in mucous membranes and the lower epidermis, the mouth is usually involved first, often before any other skin lesions, with painful erosions that interfere with eating and cause weight loss. When desmoglein-1 antibodies appear as well, skin blisters spread to the chest, back, scalp, and face. The split sits just above the basal layer, which stays anchored to the basement membrane, giving the classic row-of-tombstones look on biopsy: basal cells still standing but with nothing attached above them. Two bedside and lab findings follow from the diffuse loss of rivets: a positive Nikolsky sign, where pressure on normal-looking skin shears off a new erosion, and an intercellular fishnet pattern of IgG and C3 on direct immunofluorescence outlining each keratinocyte.



This is a type II hypersensitivity disease, with antibodies against a fixed tissue antigen, and it carries a strong tie to HLA-DR4. It is also one of the diseases that can be paraneoplastic: paraneoplastic pemphigus is linked to lymphoma, Castleman disease, and thymoma, has especially severe oral disease, and includes antibodies against several desmosomal proteins. The contrasting disease to know is bullous pemphigoid where the antibodies hit hemidesmosomes deeper down, the blisters are tense, the mucosa is usually spared, and Nikolsky is negative.



If left untreated, pemphigus vulgaris is dangerous, with high mortality from sepsis and fluid and electrolyte loss through the denuded skin. Treatment is high-dose corticosteroids to bring the disease under control, with steroid-sparing immunosuppressants such as azathioprine or mycophenolate. Rituximab, which depletes the B cells making the antibody, has become first-line for moderate-to-severe disease after trials showed it outperforms other agents, and IVIG or plasma exchange is used in refractory cases. Careful wound care is required, since superinfection of the skin with bacteria or herpes simplex is a major cause of death.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. This describes the target in bullous pemphigoid. BP180 and BP230 are hemidesmosomal proteins that anchor the epidermis to the dermis, and antibodies against them cause subepidermal, tense bullae with a negative Nikolsky sign in the elderly, the opposite of this patient's intraepidermal, flaccid, Nikolsky-positive disease.

B. In dermatitis herpetiformis IgA forms (not IgG) against epidermal transglutaminase deposits granularly at the tips of the dermal papillae, producing intensely itchy grouped vesicles tied to celiac disease, a different antibody class and a different histology.

C. Desmoglein-3, and sometimes desmoglein-1, are the desmosomal cadherins that hold keratinocytes together, and antibodies against them dissolve those connections to produce intraepidermal acantholysis with a suprabasal cleft. The mucosa-first presentation, flaccid bullae, positive Nikolsky sign, row-of-tombstones histology, and intercellular fishnet immunofluorescence all confirm pemphigus vulgaris.

D. Mucous membrane (cicatricial) pemphigoid is a subepithelial blistering disease that scars mucosae, especially the conjunctiva, with antibodies against laminin-332 or BP180. The flaccid skin blistering and intercellular IgG here are not consistent with that disease.

E. Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita is a disease where antibodies attack type VII collagen in the anchoring fibrils, causing tense bullae on trauma-prone skin with milia and scarring, which is clinically distinct from pemphigus.

Q41.  Bullous Pemphigoid

Clinical vignette

A 78-year-old woman with controlled hypertension and mild dementia presents with 8 weeks of intensely pruritic skin with urticarial plaques on the trunk and proximal extremities. Over the past 3 weeks, multiple tense fluid-filled bullae have appeared on the flexural surfaces of the arms and legs and on the trunk. The mucous membranes are spared. The bullae are TENSE and do not rupture easily; some have hemorrhagic content. Nikolsky sign is negative on perilesional skin. Biopsy of a fresh bulla shows a subepidermal cleft with an intact epidermal roof and an inflammatory infiltrate rich in eosinophils within the blister cavity and dermis. Direct immunofluorescence shows linear deposition of IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone. Salt-split skin technique (briefly soaking the biopsy in 1M NaCl, which separates epidermis from dermis at the lamina lucida) localizes the IgG to the epidermal side. Which of the following is the molecular target of the pathogenic antibodies?

Answer choices

A

Desmoglein-3 — desmosomal cadherin between keratinocytes

⊘

B

α3 chain of type IV collagen — basement membrane collagen network

⊘

C

Epidermal transglutaminase (TG3) at dermal papillae

⊘

D

Type VII collagen — anchoring fibrils in the dermis

⊘

E

BP180 (BPAG2) and BP230 (BPAG1) — hemidesmosomal proteins anchoring basal keratinocytes to the basement membrane

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Keep the brick-wall picture from the pemphigus vulgaris vignette, but move the attack downward. In pemphigus the antibodies dissolve the rivets between bricks, so the split is shallow and within the wall. In bullous pemphigoid the antibodies go after the foundation beneath the bottom row of bricks, the basement membrane zone that anchors the epidermis to the dermis. Specifically, they target hemidesmosomes, the half-rivets that tie basal keratinocytes down, with BP180 the transmembrane component and BP230 the intracellular one. When antibody binds, complement activates, eosinophils pour in, and the whole epidermis lifts off the dermis as one intact sheet. Because the roof stays whole, the blister is tense and does not rupture easily.



The clinical contrasts with pemphigus are clean and worth memorizing. Bullous pemphigoid is a disease of the elderly, often in the seventies and eighties, while pemphigus tends to hit middle age. The bullae are tense rather than flaccid, the mucosa is usually spared rather than involved first, and the Nikolsky sign is negative rather than positive. Pruritus is often dominant and can precede the blisters by weeks or months in a pre-bullous phase, whereas pemphigus is more painful than itchy. The prognosis is much better, since bullous pemphigoid is far less lethal than untreated pemphigus.



Diagnosis pairs the linear band of IgG and C3 along the basement membrane on direct immunofluorescence with the salt-split skin test. Soaking the biopsy in salt splits the skin at the lamina lucida, and in bullous pemphigoid the IgG ends up on the epidermal side (the roof) because BP180 and BP230 live there, which separates it from epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, where type VII collagen is deeper and the IgG lands on the dermal side (the floor). The histology shows the subepidermal cleft with an eosinophil-rich infiltrate, and a serum ELISA against the BP180 NC16A domain is highly specific.



The salt-split skin trick works because the basement membrane zone is layered like a sandwich, and soaking it in salt pries it apart at its weakest seam, the lamina lucida. Antigens that sit above that seam stay with the epidermal roof, and antigens below it stay with the dermal floor. BP180 and BP230 live up in the hemidesmosome, so their antibodies ride up with the roof; type VII collagen sits deeper in the anchoring fibrils, so in epidermolysis bullosa acquisita the antibody stays down on the floor. One cheap salt soak therefore sorts two diseases that otherwise look alike.



Because the disease is less severe, treatment is generally less aggressive than for pemphigus. High-potency topical corticosteroids (clobetasol) control limited disease and can be as effective and safer than systemic steroids in frail elderly patients, while oral prednisone is used for extensive disease, with doxycycline plus niacinamide, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, or rituximab as add-ons. Many cases remit over one to five years. A high-yield trigger to remember is medication-induced bullous pemphigoid, especially from DPP-4 inhibitors (the gliptins used in diabetes) and some PD-1 inhibitors.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. In pemphigus vulgaris, desmoglein-3 antibodies cause intraepidermal blistering with flaccid bullae and a positive Nikolsky sign. This patient has tense bullae, a negative Nikolsky, and a subepidermal cleft, which is pemphigoid, not pemphigus.

B. This option describes the target in anti-GBM (Goodpasture) disease, the alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen in the glomerular and alveolar basement membranes, which causes a pulmonary-renal syndrome, not a cutaneous blistering disease.

C. In dermatitis herpetiformis, IgA against epidermal transglutaminase deposits granularly at the dermal papillae, giving itchy grouped vesicles tied to celiac disease, a different antibody and a different patient.

D.  Antibodies against type VII collagen are formed in epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, this leads IgG deposition on the dermal floor of the salt-split skin, opposite to this patient's epidermal-roof pattern, and the disease favors trauma-prone areas with scarring and milia.

E. BP180 (type XVII collagen) and BP230 are hemidesmosomal proteins that anchor basal keratinocytes to the basement membrane, and antibodies against them, especially against the BP180 NC16A domain, lift the epidermis off as an intact sheet to form tense bullae. The salt-split skin localizing IgG to the epidermal roof confirms an epidermal-side antigen, distinguishing pemphigoid from epidermolysis bullosa acquisita.

References: Schmidt, E., & Zillikens, D. (2013). Pemphigoid diseases. The Lancet, 381(9863), 320–332.

Q42.  Dermatitis Herpetiformis

Clinical vignette

A 36-year-old man presents with 6 months of intensely pruritic, grouped, symmetric vesicles and excoriated papules on the extensor surfaces of his elbows, knees, scalp, and buttocks. He reports the itching is so severe at night that he cannot sleep. He has scratched the lesions so much that no intact vesicles are visible, only crusted erosions and excoriations. He reports occasional bloating and one episode of diarrhea per month, but no significant weight loss or fatigue. Laboratory studies: anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA antibodies positive at 142 U/mL (normal <20), anti-endomysial IgA antibodies positive, total IgA normal, ferritin 18 ng/mL (low-normal). Duodenal biopsy shows partial villous atrophy with increased intraepithelial lymphocytes. Skin biopsy of perilesional skin shows neutrophil-rich papillary dermal microabscesses with subepidermal blistering, and direct immunofluorescence reveals GRANULAR IgA deposits at the tips of dermal papillae. Which of the following is the most appropriate management plan?

Answer choices

A

Dapsone for immediate symptom control plus strict lifelong gluten-free diet for long-term disease modification

✓

B

Topical corticosteroids alone, applied daily to affected areas

⊘

C

High-dose systemic prednisone tapered over 8 weeks

⊘

D

Rituximab infusions every 6 months

⊘

E

Oral antihistamines for pruritus, no other therapy needed

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Dermatitis herpetiformis is the dermatological manifestation of celiac disease. The same gluten-driven immune process that damages the gut also produces an IgA antibody that cross-reacts with epidermal transglutaminase, which is the same as the tissue transglutaminase targeted in the bowel. IgA deposits in a granular pattern at the tips of the dermal papillae, pulls in neutrophils that form little microabscesses there, and lifts the skin into tiny subepidermal blisters. The result is an intensely itchy, burning eruption of grouped vesicles on the extensor surfaces, the elbows, knees, scalp, and buttocks, and it is so itchy that patients often scratch away the vesicles and present with only excoriations.



The skin-gut link runs through one family of enzymes. Transglutaminases are molecular staplers that cross-link proteins, and the gut version (tissue transglutaminase) is what the celiac immune response attacks. The skin carries a close cousin, epidermal transglutaminase, similar enough that the same antibodies grab onto it. So the eruption is not a separate allergy but the same celiac antibody finding a look-alike target in the skin, which is why no cream fixes it and why removing gluten, the upstream trigger, eventually clears both organs.



Essentially all patients with dermatitis herpetiformis have the underlying gluten-sensitive enteropathy of celiac disease, even when their bowel symptoms are mild or absent, which is why this patient has only occasional bloating yet still shows villous atrophy on duodenal biopsy. The same antibodies are positive (anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA and anti-endomysial IgA), as well as the same HLA-DQ2 and DQ8 association. Iron deficiency from malabsorption is common (the low-normal ferritin here). As with celiac testing in general, a total IgA is checked, because selective IgA deficiency would give false-negative IgA-based antibodies.



The diagnosis rests on the skin biopsy. Light microscopy shows neutrophilic microabscesses in the dermal papillae with subepidermal blistering, and direct immunofluorescence of perilesional skin shows the granular IgA at the papillary tips, which is the finding that nails it. This granular pattern separates it cleanly from the linear IgG of bullous pemphigoid and the intercellular IgG of pemphigus.



Primary treatment is with dapsone which gives fast relief to the itching and rash, often within a day or two, as it blunts neutrophil function, but it does nothing for the underlying gut disease and carries its own risks, so patients are screened for G6PD deficiency first because dapsone can cause hemolysis. The lasting fix is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, which over months treats both the skin and the enteropathy and lets the dapsone be tapered, and it also lowers the long-term risk of the enteropathy-associated lymphoma that untreated celiac disease carries. Topical steroids, systemic steroids, antihistamines, and rituximab are not the appropriate drugs here.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Dermatitis herpetiformis is managed with dapsone for rapid control of the itch and rash plus a strict lifelong gluten-free diet for long-term disease modification, since the disease is the cutaneous expression of celiac disease. The diet treats the underlying enteropathy, allows the dapsone to be tapered, and reduces the lymphoma risk, and G6PD status is checked before starting dapsone.

B. Topical corticosteroids alone do not control the disease, which is driven by a systemic gluten-triggered IgA process, and they ignore the underlying celiac enteropathy.

C. High-dose systemic prednisone is not the treatment for dermatitis herpetiformis; the disease responds to dapsone and gluten withdrawal, not to a steroid taper.

D. Rituximab depletes B cells and is used in pemphigus and other antibody diseases, but it is not the management for dermatitis herpetiformis, which is treated with dapsone and a gluten-free diet.

E. Antihistamines do little for the neutrophil-driven itch of this disease and leave both the skin and the underlying celiac enteropathy untreated.

Q43.  Vitiligo

Clinical vignette

A 30-year-old woman presents with a 1-year history of progressively enlarging white patches on the backs of her hands, around her mouth and eyes, and on her elbows. The patches are symmetric, sharply demarcated, and milky white, without scale, redness, or itching. She has a history of Hashimoto thyroiditis. Examination under a Wood lamp accentuates the depigmented areas. A skin biopsy of a patch shows a complete absence of melanocytes in the affected epidermis. Which of the following best explains the depigmentation in this patient?

Answer choices

A

Malassezia infection producing hypopigmented scaly patches

⊘

B

Autoimmune destruction of melanocytes, with melanocytes absent on biopsy

✓

C

Post-inflammatory loss of pigment after a resolved dermatitis

⊘

D

A defect in melanosome transport from giant lysosomal granules

⊘

E

A congenital tyrosinase defect with melanocytes present but unable to make melanin

⊘

Answer: B

Concept explanation

Vitiligo is the autoimmune loss of the cells that give skin its color. Melanocytes, which sit in the epidermis and make the pigment melanin, are destroyed by an immune attack led by CD8+ T cells specific for melanocyte antigens, with antibodies against melanocyte proteins such as tyrosinase also present and an IFN-gamma and CXCL10 signaling axis recruiting the T cells. As the melanocytes disappear, the skin loses pigment in well-defined patches. 



It helps to picture what a melanocyte actually does. Each one is a little pigment factory that packages melanin into granules and hands them off to the surrounding skin cells, where the pigment forms a tiny parasol over each nucleus to soak up ultraviolet light. Destroy the melanocytes and that parasol is gone, so the patch turns chalk-white and burns rather than tans in the sun. It also explains the Wood lamp: with no melanin left to absorb the ultraviolet, the bare patches throw the light back and stand out sharply against normal skin.



The patches are sharply demarcated and milky white, without scale or inflammation, and they favor the hands, the skin around the eyes and mouth, the elbows and knees, and the genitals. Two patterns exist: a common symmetric (non-segmental) form, as in this patient, which tends to be progressive and is the one tied to autoimmunity, and a segmental form confined to one area that often starts younger and stabilizes. A Wood lamp, which uses ultraviolet light, makes the depigmented areas glow and stand out, helping to map disease that is hard to see on pale skin. The Koebner phenomenon can occur, with new patches appearing at sites of skin trauma.



Vitiligo often occurs with other organ-specific autoimmune diseases, which is why this patient also has Hashimoto thyroiditis. The usual co-existing conditions are autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anemia, Addison disease, and alopecia areata, the same polyendocrine cluster seen throughout this section, so it is reasonable to check thyroid function and screen for the others over time.



In contrast to vitiligo, albinism is congenital and comes from a tyrosinase or related defect in which melanocytes are present but cannot make melanin, the opposite of vitiligo's absent melanocytes. Whereas, tinea versicolor is a Malassezia yeast infection with fine scaly hypo- or hyperpigmented patches and a spaghetti-and-meatballs appearance on KOH. Post-inflammatory hypopigmentation follows a prior rash or injury. Treatment of vitiligo aims to halt the attack and encourage repigmentation: topical corticosteroids and topical calcineurin inhibitors, narrowband UVB phototherapy, and the newer topical JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib, along with diligent sun protection, since depigmented skin burns easily and the contrast worsens with tanning.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Tinea versicolor is a Malassezia infection with fine scale and a spaghetti-and-meatballs KOH appearance. This patient's patches are non-scaly, sharply demarcated, and show absent melanocytes on biopsy, which is not an infection.

B. Vitiligo is an autoimmune destruction of melanocytes, mediated largely by CD8+ T cells with antibodies against melanocyte antigens, and the biopsy shows an absence of melanocytes in the affected skin. The symmetric, sharply demarcated milky-white patches, the Wood lamp accentuation, and the coexisting Hashimoto thyroiditis all fit.

C. Post-inflammatory hypopigmentation follows a prior dermatitis or injury and is usually less sharply demarcated. There is no preceding rash here, and the biopsy shows complete melanocyte loss with the autoimmune associations of vitiligo.

D. In Chediak-Higashi syndrome, a lysosomal trafficking defect produces giant granules and impaired melanosome transport along with immunodeficiency and bleeding. That is a congenital multisystem disease, not the isolated autoimmune melanocyte loss of vitiligo.

E. Albinism is a congenital tyrosinase (or related) defect in which melanocytes are present but cannot synthesize melanin. This patient's biopsy shows absent melanocytes and her depigmentation is acquired and patchy, not a congenital, generalized inability to make pigment.

A

Smoking causes secondary surfactant deficiency leading to alveolar collapse and bleeding

⊘

B

Anti-GBM antibodies preferentially target a different collagen isoform in lung versus kidney

⊘

C

Cigarette smoking damages the alveolar capillary endothelium, exposing the α3 chain of type IV collagen in the alveolar basement membrane to circulating anti-GBM antibodies

✓

D

⊘

E

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Anti-glomerular basement membrane disease, previously called Goodpasture syndrome, is one of the few diseases where a single autoantibody directly targets two distant organs. The antibody targets a protein tucked inside the basement membrane, the thin sheet of matrix that sits beneath every epithelial and endothelial layer. The exact target is the non-collagenous domain of the alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen. That chain is found in only a few places, most prominently the glomerular basement membrane of the kidney and the alveolar basement membrane of the lung, so the disease zeroes in on exactly those two organs and produces a pulmonary-renal syndrome.



In the kidney, the glomerular capillaries are fenestrated, meaning they have built-in holes, so circulating antibody reaches the basement membrane with ease, and anti-GBM disease therefore almost always causes glomerulonephritis when the antibody is present. In the lung, the alveolar capillaries have a continuous endothelium that normally shields the alveolar basement membrane from circulating antibody, so the antibody passes through without touching its target, which is why many patients have isolated kidney disease. To get lung disease, the alveolar endothelium has to be injured first so the antibody can reach the membrane. Smoking is the single most important injury, since it directly damages the alveolar capillary lining and raises its permeability, and other triggers include a viral respiratory infection, inhaled hydrocarbons, oxygen toxicity, and volume overload. This patient's 14 pack-years is a major risk factor for adding pulmonary hemorrhage to the renal disease.



The laboratory investigations separate Goodpasture from the other pulmonary-renal syndromes. The hallmark is linear IgG along the glomerular basement membrane on immunofluorescence, which contrasts with the lumpy-bumpy granular pattern of immune-complex disease such as IgA nephropathy and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Anti-GBM antibodies (usually IgG1) are highly specific, and the ANCA panel is usually negative, though a minority of patients are double-positive for both anti-GBM and ANCA. The renal biopsy shows crescentic glomerulonephritis, with proliferating cells filling Bowman space and crushing the glomeruli.



The linear-versus-granular distinction is worth fixing in mind, because it recurs across kidney disease. When the antibody is aimed at the basement membrane itself, it coats the membrane evenly, like a fresh coat of paint along a wall, and immunofluorescence lights up as a smooth linear line, the fingerprint of anti-GBM disease. When instead pre-formed immune complexes float in and drop out of the blood, they land in irregular clumps, so the stain looks lumpy and granular. Reading smooth versus lumpy tells you whether the antibody targets the wall or just litters it.



Left untreated, anti-GBM disease is fulminant, with high mortality at one year from renal failure or pulmonary hemorrhage, so treatment is urgent beginning with plasmapheresis to physically strip the circulating antibody, high-dose corticosteroids, and cyclophosphamide or rituximab to shut down new antibody production, and mandatory smoking cessation. 

Why each option is right or wrong

A. The bleeding in Goodpasture is antibody-mediated alveolar hemorrhage from capillary injury, not surfactant deficiency. Surfactant problems belong to neonatal respiratory distress and some other conditions, not this disease.

B. The antibody targets the same alpha-3 chain in both organs; the difference in involvement comes from access (the alveolar endothelium blocks antibody, the glomerular capillaries do not), not from different antigens.

C. The alpha-3 chain of type IV collagen is the antibody target in both kidney and lung. The kidney's fenestrated glomerular capillaries let antibody reach the membrane easily, so renal disease is the rule, while the lung's continuous alveolar endothelium normally blocks access. Smoking damages that barrier and exposes the alveolar collagen to circulating antibody, which is why pulmonary hemorrhage shows up mainly in smokers, and this patient's 14 pack-years fits.

D. Hypertensive emergency can cause pulmonary edema, but that gives perihilar edema and effusions, not the diffuse alveolar hemorrhage and crescentic glomerulonephritis with linear IgG seen here.

E.  This option names the other classic pulmonary-renal syndrome, ANCA-associated vasculitis, which causes a pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis with scant deposits. This patient's biopsy shows linear IgG and the ANCA panel is negative, which points to anti-GBM disease rather than an ANCA vasculitis.

Q45.  IgA Nephropathy (Berger Disease)

Clinical vignette

A 24-year-old previously healthy man presents to the emergency department with 12 hours of cola-colored urine. He reports the urine change began approximately 36 hours after the onset of a sore throat and low-grade fever, which he attributed to a viral upper respiratory infection. He recalls one similar episode of dark urine 18 months ago that occurred 2 days into a respiratory illness and resolved on its own within a week. He has no medications, no edema, no flank pain, no rash. Vital signs: BP 138/86 mmHg, otherwise normal. Examination: mild pharyngeal erythema, no rash, no joint swelling. Laboratory studies: serum creatinine 1.1 mg/dL (normal), C3 normal, C4 normal, ASO titer normal, serum IgA mildly elevated. Urinalysis: 3+ blood with dysmorphic RBCs and rare red cell casts, 1+ protein, no glucose. 24-hour urine protein 480 mg (mildly elevated). Renal biopsy shows mesangial proliferation with prominent IgA deposits in the mesangium on immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy shows electron-dense deposits in the mesangium. Which of the following clinical and laboratory features best distinguishes this disease from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis?

Answer choices

A

Presence of hematuria with red blood cell casts and dysmorphic red blood cells

⊘

B

Elevated blood pressure during the acute episode

⊘

C

History of upper respiratory infection preceding the renal symptoms

⊘

D

Synpharyngitic hematuria (1–3 days after upper respiratory infection), normal serum complement (normal C3 and C4), and isolated IgA-dominant mesangial deposits on biopsy

✓

E

Mild proteinuria below the nephrotic range

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

Think of the kidney's filtering units as fine mesh sieves that need to stay clean to filter blood. In IgA nephropathy the body makes a faulty version of IgA, specifically IgA1 with poorly attached sugars on its hinge region, called galactose-deficient IgA1. Other antibodies in the blood recognize this abnormal IgA1 as foreign and form immune complexes with it. Those complexes settle in the mesangium, the central scaffold of the glomerulus, activate complement, and stir up inflammation and mesangial proliferation. The mesh gums up, red cells leak across the damaged filter, and the patient passes cola-colored urine.

The timing of that hematuria is the most testable feature, and it has a name: synpharyngitic hematuria. 



The visible blood appears one to three days after an upper respiratory infection begins (sometimes after gastroenteritis or hard exercise), because mucosal infection drives IgA production at mucosal sites, including the defective galactose-deficient IgA1, which spills into the circulation almost at once. This is the clean distinction from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, where hematuria appears one to three weeks after a strep infection because the immune system needs that long to mount a response to streptococcal antigens. IgA nephropathy is synpharyngitic (during the infection) while post-streptococcal disease is post-streptococcal (weeks after). Two lab differences reinforce this disctinction; IgA nephropathy has normal complement (normal C3 and C4), while post-streptococcal disease has a low C3 from alternative-pathway activation, and the ASO titer is raised in post-streptococcal disease and normal here.



The timing difference comes down to old supply versus new manufacturing. In IgA nephropathy the faulty IgA1 is already circulating, and a fresh mucosal infection just cranks the same mucosal factories into overdrive, so the bad antibody surges within a day or two and the urine darkens almost in step with the sore throat. Post-streptococcal disease, by contrast, has to build an immune response from scratch against the strep, make antibody, form complexes, and let them settle, which takes one to three weeks. Same trigger, but one reuses stock on hand and the other has to tool up.



The diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy showing IgA-dominant deposits in the mesangium on immunofluorescence. About half of patients have isolated hematuria with little proteinuria and normal function and an excellent prognosis, while the other half have proteinuria, hypertension, and progressive chronic kidney disease, with a meaningful fraction reaching end-stage disease over decades. IgA nephropathy is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and is especially common in East Asia. Its systemic cousin is IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schonlein purpura), which adds palpable purpura, abdominal pain, and arthralgia to the same IgA-deposit mechanism.



Treatment is risk-stratified. Minimal disease (isolated hematuria, no proteinuria, normal pressure) needs only monitoring. Proteinuria above roughly 0.5 to 1 gram a day or hypertension is treated first with renin-angiotensin blockade (an ACE inhibitor or ARB). Progressive disease may get corticosteroids, though recent trials showed only modest benefit against a real infection risk, and a newer targeted-release budesonide that acts on the gut Peyer patches where the defective IgA1 is made is now used in high-risk disease.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Hematuria with red cell casts and dysmorphic red cells reflects glomerular bleeding and occurs in both diseases, so it cannot tell them apart.

B. Both diseases can raise blood pressure during an acute episode, and both can be mild and normotensive, so blood pressure does not distinguish them.

C. Preceding upper respiratory infection is common to both diseases, which is exactly the source of confusion. The distinguishing factor is the timing relative to that infection.

D. Synpharyngitic timing (one to three days after the infection rather than one to three weeks), normal C3 and C4 (rather than the low C3 of post-streptococcal disease), and IgA-dominant mesangial deposits on biopsy, distinguish IgA nephropathy from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. The timing alone is often enough: this patient's cola-colored urine started 36 hours into his sore throat.

E. Proteinuria occurs in both, and post-streptococcal disease also tends to have low-grade proteinuria with hematuria, so the amount does not separate them.

Q46.  Lupus Nephritis (Class IV, Diffuse Proliferative)

Clinical vignette

A 27-year-old woman with established SLE (diagnosed 3 years ago on the basis of malar rash, arthritis, anti-Smith antibodies, and previous Class II lupus nephritis) and currently on maintenance hydroxychloroquine, presents with a 6-week course of progressive ankle and periorbital edema, foamy urine, fatigue, and headaches. Anti-dsDNA 480 IU/mL (markedly elevated from baseline 80), C3 38 mg/dL (low), C4 6 mg/dL (low), ANA strongly positive. Renal biopsy is performed and shows diffuse proliferative glomerulonephritis (involving >50% of glomeruli) with crescents in 30% of glomeruli, subendothelial 'wire-loop' deposits visible on light microscopy, and immunofluorescence demonstrating a 'full house' pattern (IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C4, and C1q all positive). Which of the following is the most appropriate induction treatment for this patient?

Answer choices

A

ACE inhibitor monotherapy with hydroxychloroquine continuation

⊘

B

Low-dose methotrexate as single agent

⊘

C

Therapeutic plasma exchange as first-line monotherapy

⊘

D

Renal transplantation listing without immunosuppressant induction

⊘

E

Mycophenolate mofetil (or IV cyclophosphamide) PLUS high-dose corticosteroids (pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone)

✓

Answer: E

Concept explanation

Lupus nephritis is the renal manifestation of SLE, and it is one of the strongest drivers of long-term outcome in lupus. The mechanism is a type III hypersensitivity (immune-complex) process: anti-dsDNA antibodies bind DNA in the circulation, the complexes deposit in the glomerulus, they fix complement (consuming both C3 and C4, which is why both fall together in lupus, unlike post-streptococcal disease where mainly C3 drops), and they recruit inflammatory cells that damage the filter. The result ranges from mild proteinuria to a combined nephritic-nephrotic picture with failing kidneys. The kidney is involved at some point in most lupus patients, and clinically meaningful nephritis affects a large minority.



The complement pattern is a quiet but reliable clue. Immune complexes switch on the classical complement pathway, which burns through C4 first and then C3, so in active lupus nephritis both numbers drop together. Diseases that instead trip the alternative pathway, like post-streptococcal nephritis, chew through C3 while leaving C4 roughly intact. So a low C3 with a low C4 points at an immune-complex, classical-pathway disease such as lupus, and watching those numbers climb back up is one way to tell that treatment is working.



Pathologists classify lupus nephritis onto six classes based on where the complexes deposit and how the glomerulus responds. Class I is minimal mesangial disease, Class II is mesangial proliferation, Class III is focal proliferative disease (under half the glomeruli), Class IV is diffuse proliferative disease (over half the glomeruli, the most aggressive and the most common biopsy class), Class V is membranous disease with subepithelial deposits and nephrotic-range proteinuria, and Class VI is advanced sclerosis. Two findings flag the aggressive proliferative classes: the wire-loop deposits, thickened capillary walls from heavy subendothelial deposits visible on light microscopy, and the full-house immunofluorescence pattern, staining positive for IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C4, and C1q all at once, which is the signature of lupus nephritis.



Treatment of Class III and IV disease follows an induction phase. Induction, over three to six months, pairs high-dose corticosteroids (pulse IV methylprednisolone, then a tapering oral course) with an immunosuppressant. Two induction agents are standard: mycophenolate mofetil, often preferred in younger women, in Black and Hispanic patients where it outperforms cyclophosphamide, and where fertility matters; and IV cyclophosphamide. Maintenance therapy uses mycophenolate or azathioprine to prevent flares. Hydroxychloroquine is continued in everyone with lupus because it cuts flares and improves outcomes.



Several newer agents now add on to the treatment regimen. Voclosporin (a calcineurin inhibitor) combined with mycophenolate, belimumab (against the B-cell survival factor BAFF), and anifrolumab (against the type I interferon receptor), with rituximab kept for refractory disease. The goal is a complete renal response by about twelve months, which predicts good long-term kidney survival. The steroid quickly damps the active inflammation while the immunosuppressant secures a durable remission, and adjuncts like an ACE inhibitor or ARB are added to reduce proteinuria.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. ACE inhibitor monotherapy reduces proteinuria in mild (Class I or II) disease but cannot control the active immune-complex inflammation of Class IV disease, so used alone it would let this patient progress to end-stage kidney disease. It belongs alongside induction immunosuppression.

B. Methotrexate helps lupus arthritis and skin disease but is not effective for proliferative lupus nephritis and accumulates dangerously when the creatinine is already raised, as here. The induction agents are mycophenolate or cyclophosphamide.

C. Plasma exchange is the treatment for anti-GBM disease (Q44) and TTP, not routine lupus nephritis. It is reserved for catastrophic lupus with diffuse alveolar hemorrhage or severe thrombotic microangiopathy, not first-line induction for Class IV disease.

D. Class IV disease with heavy proteinuria and a rising creatinine has a real chance of recovery with prompt induction, and many patients reach a complete response within a year. Transplantation is for those who progress to end-stage disease despite optimal therapy, and ideally during quiescence, since transplanting an actively flaring patient risks recurrence in the graft.

E. Class IV proliferative lupus nephritis progresses to end-stage disease without aggressive treatment, and the standard of care is induction with high-dose corticosteroids plus either mycophenolate mofetil or IV cyclophosphamide, supported by strong trial evidence. For a young woman planning pregnancy, mycophenolate is generally preferred over cyclophosphamide to spare fertility (though mycophenolate is itself teratogenic and is stopped before conception), and hydroxychloroquine continues throughout.

Laboratory studies: serum albumin 2.4 g/dL, total cholesterol markedly elevated, serum creatinine 1.0 mg/dL (normal), and a 24-hour urine protein of 7.2 g with lipiduria and oval fat bodies but only minimal blood. Renal biopsy shows diffuse thickening of the glomerular basement membrane without proliferation; electron microscopy shows subepithelial electron-dense deposits with projections of basement membrane between them ("spike-and-dome"), and immunofluorescence shows granular IgG and C3 along the capillary wall. Which of the following findings is most characteristic of this patient's disease?

Answer choices

A

Anti-PLA2R antibodies with subepithelial immune deposits producing a "spike-and-dome" pattern

✓

⊘

⊘

Answer: A

Concept explanation

Membranous nephropathy is a leading cause of primary nephrotic syndrome in adults, especially in older adults. The problem sits at the outer edge of the glomerular filter, on the podocyte side of the basement membrane. In the primary form the body makes an antibody against a protein on the podocyte surface, the phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R), in roughly three-quarters of cases, with a smaller share against THSD7A. The antibody binds its target right where the podocyte meets the basement membrane, the immune complexes form there, and they sit as subepithelial deposits that damage the filter and let protein pour out.



Because the injury is to the filtration barrier rather than a proliferative inflammation, the clinical picture is nephrotic rather than nephritic; heavy proteinuria above 3.5 grams a day, low serum albumin, edema, high cholesterol, and fatty casts in the urine, with little blood and a normal complement (the deposits are subepithelial and do not consume circulating complement the way lupus does). This patient shows the full nephrotic syndrome with a normal creatinine and normal C3 and C4.



Where the deposits sit decides whether the kidney bleeds or leaks. Deposits on the inner, blood-facing side of the filter (subendothelial, as in lupus) sit right in the traffic of circulating cells and complement, so they ignite inflammation, a nephritic picture with blood in the urine and falling complement. Membranous deposits sit on the far, urine side of the membrane (subepithelial), walled off from the bloodstream, so they quietly wreck the filter's sieve without drawing a crowd: heavy protein loss, little blood, and a normal complement. Same kind of immune complexes, opposite clinical face, depending only on which side of the wall they land.



Light microscopy shows diffuse thickening of the glomerular basement membrane without cell proliferation. Immunofluorescence shows granular IgG and C3 along the capillary wall, the lumpy-bumpy pattern of immune-complex disease. Electron microscopy shows subepithelial deposits with new basement membrane growing up between them as spikes, the spike-and-dome appearance. A silver stain highlights those spikes. This is in contrast with the other nephrotic patterns: minimal change disease shows only foot-process effacement with no deposits, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis shows segmental scarring, and membranous shows the subepithelial deposits and spikes.



Two practical points round it out. First, membranous nephropathy carries the highest risk of hypercoagulability among the nephrotic syndromes, with renal vein thrombosis its classic complication (along with pulmonary embolism), partly from urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins, so new flank pain or a falling kidney function should prompt a look for it. Second, the disease can be primary (anti-PLA2R-driven) or secondary, so a workup looks for solid tumors (lung, colon, prostate), hepatitis B, lupus (membranous lupus nephritis is Class V), and drugs such as NSAIDs. Treatment is supportive for everyone (renin-angiotensin blockade, a statin, and anticoagulation when the risk is high), with immunosuppression for higher-risk disease, where rituximab has become first-line and a cyclophosphamide-plus-steroid regimen is an alternative, and anti-PLA2R titers can be followed to track disease activity.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Primary membranous nephropathy is driven by anti-PLA2R antibodies, and the biopsy shows subepithelial immune deposits with basement-membrane spikes between them, the spike-and-dome pattern, along with diffuse basement-membrane thickening and granular IgG and C3. That fits this patient's nephrotic syndrome with normal complement and a positive anti-PLA2R.

B. Anti-GBM disease presents with linear IgG along the basement membrane and a pulmonary-renal, nephritic picture. This patient has granular deposits and a nephrotic syndrome, not linear staining.

C. IgA nephropathy presents with mesangial IgA deposits and a nephritic, synpharyngitic-hematuria presentation, not the subepithelial deposits and heavy proteinuria of membranous disease.

D. Proliferative lupus nephritis contains a full-house immunofluorescence and subendothelial wire-loop deposits and a low complement. This patient has subepithelial deposits, granular IgG and C3 (not full-house), and normal complement, which is membranous nephropathy.

E. In minimal change disease, the only finding is podocyte foot-process effacement with no immune deposits, typically in children. This patient has clear subepithelial deposits and basement-membrane spikes, which is membranous nephropathy.

Q48.  Warm Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia

Clinical vignette

A 52-year-old woman with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL, observed without treatment for 3 years) presents with 2 weeks of progressive fatigue, exertional dyspnea, dark urine, and yellowing of her eyes. Examination: pale conjunctivae, scleral icterus, palpable splenomegaly. Laboratory studies: hemoglobin 6.8 g/dL (down from 11.4 g/dL 3 months ago), mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 108 fL, reticulocyte count 9.6% (markedly elevated, indicating active marrow response), LDH 940 U/L (elevated), total bilirubin 4.8 mg/dL with indirect (unconjugated) fraction 3.9 mg/dL, haptoglobin <10 mg/dL (suppressed). Peripheral blood smear shows numerous spherocytes (small dense round red blood cells without central pallor), polychromasia (reflecting reticulocytosis), and occasional schistocytes are absent. direct antiglobulin test (Coombs) is strongly positive for IgG with weak C3 positivity. indirect antiglobulin test is also positive. Which of the following best explains the mechanism of red cell destruction in this patient?

Answer choices

A

Mechanical shearing of red cells passing through fibrin strands in microvessels (microangiopathic hemolytic anemia)

⊘

B

IgG antibodies bind red cell surface antigens at body temperature (37 °C); macrophages in the splenic red pulp recognize the IgG-coated cells via Fcγ receptors and remove them by EXTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

✓

C

Membrane defect in red cell cytoskeleton (hereditary spherocytosis with positive family history)

⊘

D

Oxidative damage to hemoglobin (G6PD deficiency)

⊘

E

IgM antibodies bind red cell I antigen at cold temperatures, activate complement, and cause INTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

⊘

Answer: B

Concept explanation

The spleen is like a quality-control checkpoint that inspects every red cell passing through. Healthy cells are flexible and slip through the splenic cords, while damaged or antibody-coated cells get caught and examined by macrophages in the red pulp. When a macrophage finds a cell coated with IgG, it grabs the antibody tails through its Fc-gamma receptors and tears off a piece of membrane. The cell loses surface area, rounds up into a spherocyte, and on its next pass either gets eaten whole or is too stiff to squeeze through and is trapped. This is extravascular hemolysis, destruction in the reticuloendothelial system, mostly the spleen. In warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia the body makes IgG antibodies against its own red cell antigens (often in the Rh system), the IgG coats cells throughout the bloodstream, the spleen removes them with each pass, and anemia builds up.



Why a nibbled cell becomes a sphere is just geometry. A normal red cell is a flat disc with spare, floppy membrane, which is what lets it fold and squeeze through tiny vessels. When a splenic macrophage bites off a piece of membrane but leaves the cell's contents intact, the cell has less skin to wrap the same volume, and the shape that holds the most volume in the least surface is a ball. So it tightens into a stiff little sphere, loses the central pale dimple, and on its next pass through the spleen cannot deform enough to escape, which is why spherocytes mean a cell is being eaten piecemeal.



There are two types of autoimmune hemolytic anemia, split by antibody class and the temperature at which the antibody is active, and the split decides the mechanism, the smear, the complement story, and the treatment. Warm disease uses IgG active at body temperature, shows spherocytes on the smear, destroys cells extravascularly in the spleen, and arises idiopathically or secondary to CLL, lupus, or drugs such as methyldopa, penicillin, and cephalosporins. Cold disease uses IgM, is active in the cold, shows red cell clumping on the smear, and is complement-driven.



The lab clues of any hemolysis are present here: a high indirect bilirubin, a high LDH, a low haptoglobin (used up binding free hemoglobin), and a brisk reticulocytosis as the marrow responds, often with splenomegaly. The test that pins the root cause is the direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, where the patient's washed red cells are mixed with anti-IgG and anti-C3: agglutination shows what is coating the cells. A pattern positive for IgG (with or without C3) means warm disease, while a C3-only pattern means cold disease, because the IgM has dissociated by the time the test runs at room temperature. This patient's IgG-positive Coombs with spherocytes is squarely warm disease.



Treatment differs sharply between the two. Warm disease responds to corticosteroids first-line (they blunt macrophage Fc-receptor function and antibody production), with rituximab second-line and splenectomy or other immunosuppressants for refractory cases. Cold disease responds poorly to steroids and is treated with rituximab, the anti-C1s antibody sutimlimab, and cold avoidance. Either form can be primary or secondary, so the workup always looks for an underlying lymphoproliferative disorder (CLL or lymphoma), lupus, infection, or a drug.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. In microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (as in TTP, HUS, DIC, or scleroderma renal crisis), the smear shows schistocytes and the Coombs is negative because the destruction is mechanical. This patient has spherocytes, no schistocytes, and a positive Coombs.

B. Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia comes from IgG autoantibodies binding red cells at body temperature, with splenic macrophages recognizing the IgG through Fc-gamma receptors and removing the cells extravascularly, producing spherocytes. The spherocytes, high indirect bilirubin and LDH, low haptoglobin, reticulocytosis, splenomegaly, and an IgG-positive Coombs all fit, and the CLL background is the classic secondary cause.

C. Hereditary spherocytosis is an inherited cytoskeletal defect that also makes spherocytes but is Coombs-negative because no antibody is involved. This patient's positive Coombs marks an acquired, antibody-mediated process.

D. In patients with G6PD deficiency, oxidative stress produces bite cells and Heinz bodies, not spherocytes, with a negative Coombs. That does not fit this patient's findings.

E. This option is the cold agglutinin disease, with IgM against the I antigen activating complement in the cold. That smear shows agglutination rather than spherocytes and the Coombs is typically C3-only, unlike this patient's IgG-positive, spherocyte picture.

References: Brodsky, R. A. (2019). Warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia. New England Journal of Medicine, 381(7), 647–654. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp1900554

A month earlier he had an atypical pneumonia treated as an outpatient. Examination shows pallor, mild scleral icterus, and dusky discoloration of the fingertips. Laboratory studies: hemoglobin 8.6 g/dL, elevated LDH, elevated indirect bilirubin, low haptoglobin, and reticulocytosis. The peripheral smear shows clumping (agglutination) of red blood cells at room temperature. The direct antiglobulin test is positive for C3 only and negative for IgG, and a cold agglutinin titer is high. Which of the following best explains the mechanism of red cell destruction in this patient?

Answer choices

A

IgG antibodies bind red cells at body temperature, with splenic Fcγ-mediated extravascular removal

⊘

B

An inherited red cell cytoskeleton defect producing spherocytes

⊘

C

IgM antibodies bind the red cell I antigen at cold temperatures and activate complement, with C3-mediated clearance

✓

D

Oxidative damage to hemoglobin from an enzyme deficiency

⊘

E

Mechanical shearing of red cells through fibrin strands in small vessels

⊘

Answer: C

Concept explanation

Cold agglutinin disease is the cold-loving sibling of warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, and the difference here is with the antibody. Here the antibody is IgM, directed against the I antigen on red cells, and it binds best at cold temperatures. IgM is a large pentamer, so it can bridge several red cells at once, which makes them clump (agglutinate), and this happens most in the coolest parts of the body, the fingers, toes, ears, and nose. That cold-dependent clumping in the periphery produces the dusky, aching, bluish digits (acrocyanosis) that this patient notices in the cold which later gets better on warming.



The destruction is complement-driven, which is the underlying mechanism of the disease. IgM is the most efficient complement activator, so once it binds in the cold and the blood returns to the warmer core, the antibody falls off but leaves complement behind. C3b coats the cells and tags them for clearance by Kupffer cells in the liver, which is the main route, with a smaller amount of direct intravascular lysis by the membrane attack complex. The usual hemolysis labs follow: a high LDH, a high indirect bilirubin, a low haptoglobin, and reticulocytosis.



Two quirks of IgM explain the whole disease. First, IgM is a five-armed pentamer, so a single molecule can plant several binding sites at once and snap complement's first component (C1q) into action far more efficiently than a lone IgG can, which is why the damage here is complement-driven rather than macrophage-driven. Second, because IgM grips only in the cold and lets go as blood rewarms in the body's core, the antibody itself is usually gone by the time blood is drawn, leaving only the C3b it deposited, which is exactly why the Coombs test reads C3-positive but IgG-negative. The C3b-tagged cells are then cleared mostly by the liver's macrophages rather than the spleen.



Acute, secondary cold agglutinin disease classically follows Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (with an anti-I antibody) or EBV mononucleosis (with an anti-i antibody), and this patient's recent atypical pneumonia fits Mycoplasma. Chronic, primary cold agglutinin disease is a disease of older adults and is often tied to an underlying low-grade lymphoproliferative disorder, so a search for lymphoma or a monoclonal process is part of the workup. On the smear you see red cell agglutination rather than the spherocytes of warm disease, and the direct antiglobulin test is positive for C3 only and negative for IgG, because the IgM has dissociated by the time the test runs at room temperature.



Treatment is different from warm AIHA. Corticosteroids and splenectomy work poorly here, because the clearance is complement-driven and liver-based rather than spleen-and-Fc-receptor-based. The approach to management is cold avoidance with warming of the patient and of all IV fluids and blood products, treatment of any underlying infection or lymphoma, rituximab as first-line drug therapy, and the newer anti-C1s antibody sutimlimab, which blocks the classical complement pathway at its start.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. In warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia the IgG is at body temperature and there is splenic Fc-gamma-mediated extravascular removal producing spherocytes and an IgG-positive Coombs. This patient has agglutination, a C3-only Coombs, and cold-dependent symptoms, which is cold disease.

B. Hereditary spherocytosis is an inherited membrane defect that is Coombs-negative and makes spherocytes, not the acquired, cold-active, complement-mediated agglutination seen here.

C. Cold agglutinin disease is caused by IgM antibodies against the red cell I antigen that bind in the cold and activate complement, leading to C3b-mediated clearance in the liver. The cold-induced acrocyanosis, red cell agglutination on the smear, a C3-only and IgG-negative Coombs, a high cold agglutinin titer, and the preceding Mycoplasma pneumonia all fit.

D. G6PD deficiency occurs due to oxidative stress which causes bite cells and Heinz bodies with a negative Coombs, this does not match this patient's findings.

E. In microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, schistocytes and a negative Coombs from mechanical shearing are found, not the antibody-and-complement, agglutinating picture here.

References: Berentsen, S., & Barcellini, W. (2021). Autoimmune hemolytic anemias. New England Journal of Medicine, 385(15), 1407–1419. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra2033982

Q50.  Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Clinical vignette

A 29-year-old woman presents with 2 weeks of easy bruising, pinpoint red spots on her shins, bleeding gums when brushing, and one episode of a nosebleed. She feels otherwise well and takes no medications. Examination shows scattered petechiae on the lower legs and a few ecchymoses, with no lymphadenopathy and no splenomegaly. Laboratory studies: platelet count 14,000/μL, with a normal hemoglobin and white blood cell count. The peripheral smear shows reduced platelet numbers with some large platelets and no schistocytes, and the red and white cells look normal. Coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen) are normal. Which of the following best explains this patient's findings?

Answer choices

A

Consumption of platelets and clotting factors with prolonged PT and aPTT and low fibrinogen

⊘

B

ADAMTS13 deficiency with platelet microthrombi and schistocytes on the smear

⊘

C

Antiphospholipid antibodies causing thrombosis with secondary thrombocytopenia

⊘

D

IgG autoantibodies against platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa, with splenic macrophage destruction of platelets and impaired platelet production

✓

E

Bone marrow infiltration replacing megakaryocytes

⊘

Answer: D

Concept explanation

Immune thrombocytopenia is an autoimmune attack on platelets. The body makes IgG autoantibodies against platelet surface glycoproteins, most often GpIIb/IIIa (and sometimes GpIb/IX). The antibody-coated platelets are then recognized by splenic macrophages through their Fc-gamma receptors and removed, the same extravascular mechanism that destroys red cells in warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia, but this time it is just aimed at platelets. There is a second attack as well, the same antibodies bind megakaryocytes in the marrow and blunt platelet production, so the platelet count falls both because platelets are cleared faster and because fewer are made.



The clinical picture is one of platelet-type (mucocutaneous) bleeding. Patients get petechiae, easy bruising, bleeding gums, epistaxis, and heavy menses, and the dangerous but uncommon event is intracranial hemorrhage at very low counts. This contrasts with the deep bleeding (hemarthroses, muscle hematomas) of clotting-factor disorders. The defining laboratory feature is that the thrombocytopenia is isolated: the platelet count is low while the hemoglobin and white count are normal, the smear shows reduced platelets (often with some large young platelets) and otherwise normal cells with no schistocytes, and the coagulation studies are normal



The kind of bleeding tells you which part of clotting failed. Platelets handle the first, fast patch, plugging the constant tiny leaks in skin and mucous membranes, so when they run low the bleeding is superficial and pinpoint: petechiae, bruises, gums, nosebleeds, heavy periods. Clotting factors build the slower, deeper, reinforcing mesh over that platelet plug, so when they are missing (as in hemophilia) the bleeding is deep, into joints and muscles. Spotting petechiae and mucosal oozing rather than joint bleeds tells you the problem is platelets, which is the pattern in this patient.



Immune thrombocytopenia is largely a diagnosis of exclusion, so the reasoning is about ruling out the identical diseases. There is no microangiopathy, so no schistocytes and a normal LDH argue against TTP and HUS. The coagulation studies are normal and the fibrinogen is intact, which points against DIC. The other cell lines are normal, which rules out marrow infiltration by leukemia or aplastic anemia. The course differs by age; in children it is often acute and follows a viral infection and resolves on its own, while in adults it tends to be chronic and is more common in women. It can be primary or secondary to lupus, CLL, HIV, hepatitis C, or Helicobacter pylori, so those are checked.



Treatment depends on the count and bleeding. Mild, asymptomatic disease can simply be observed. When treatment is needed, corticosteroids are first-line, and IVIG works quickly when a rapid rise is needed (for active bleeding or before a procedure) by saturating macrophage Fc receptors, with anti-D an option in Rh-positive, non-splenectomized patients. Second-line options include rituximab, the thrombopoietin-receptor agonists eltrombopag and romiplostim, and splenectomy. Platelet transfusions are saved for life-threatening bleeding, since transfused platelets are also cleared quickly.

Why each option is right or wrong

A. Disseminated intravascular coagulation is a consumptive process that prolongs the PT and aPTT and lowers fibrinogen, usually in a sick patient. This patient's coagulation studies and fibrinogen are normal, which is not DIC.

B. In thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, there is ADAMTS13 deficiency, with platelet microthrombi and a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia showing schistocytes. This patient has no schistocytes and a normal hemoglobin, so it is not TTP.

C. In antiphospholipid syndrome, there can be a lower platelet count but it usually occurs in the setting of thrombosis and pregnancy loss with antiphospholipid antibodies. This patient has isolated bleeding, not clotting, which fits immune thrombocytopenia rather than antiphospholipid syndrome.

D. Immune thrombocytopenia is caused by IgG autoantibodies against platelet GpIIb/IIIa, leading to splenic macrophage destruction of platelets and reduced megakaryocyte output. The isolated thrombocytopenia with mucocutaneous bleeding, a normal hemoglobin and white count, a smear with reduced platelets and no schistocytes, and normal coagulation studies all fit.

E. This option describes marrow infiltration (as in leukemia) or aplasia, which would usually drop the other cell lines as well. This patient has an isolated thrombocytopenia with a normal hemoglobin and white count, which argues against a marrow-replacement process.

References: Cooper, N., & Ghanima, W. (2019). Immune thrombocytopenia. New England Journal of Medicine, 381(10), 945–955. https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMcp1810479
